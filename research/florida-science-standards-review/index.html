A visual guide to the review

What changed in Florida’s proposed science standards?

Florida is reviewing new K–12 science standards. This independent comparison looks at what the 2026 draft teaches, what it leaves out, and how it relates to earlier standards and public records.

These are proposals, not adopted standards. This review is independent of the Florida Department of Education. It describes the documents examined and does not establish who wrote individual benchmarks or why. The draft foreword says clarifications and examples help define expected outcomes and carry the full weight of the proposed standards. We show their scale separately from coded-text comparisons; final legal status depends on the rule and its incorporated files. How the source layers and adoption status are treated (new tab).

01 / Size and expectations

The draft includes more than its numbered benchmarks

The FDOE foreword says the benchmarks, clarifications, and examples together represent expected student outcomes and “carry the full weight of the standards.” The expansions matter to the proposal’s intended instructional scope. The 2026 files are still a review draft; final legal status depends on the rule and the materials it adopts. Read the proposed foreword (new tab).

Two useful size measures answer different questions. The coded parent-standard and benchmark wording is about 25.6% longer than the same coded layer in Florida’s 2008 standards. The whole 2026 review packet is much larger because it also contains expanded clarifications, examples, student tasks, the foreword, tables, headings, repeated labels, and layout text. Whole-packet length measures source-document volume, not the number of binding requirements, unique learning targets, or teaching hours.

In short: the coded Florida comparison shows a moderate text increase; the complete 2026 proposal packet is substantially larger because it carries extensive benchmark-linked material. FDOE’s foreword makes those clarifications and examples important to review, while the packet-wide token total still includes text that is not itself a learning expectation.

02 / The headline findings

Evolution and climate change are largely unnamed

Both subjects have related science in the draft. The concern is that major explanations are not stated in the coded standards and numbered benchmark statements. Clarifications and examples are analyzed separately because their status in a final rule must be checked.

Evolution

Mechanisms appear; the unifying explanation does not

No numbered benchmark uses “evolution” biologically; one parent standard uses “landscape evolution” geologically. The coded text does not name common ancestry as the explanation connecting evidence, or retain the 2008 human-evolution expectations.

Climate change

One clarification mention; none in coded standards

“Climate change” appears once in a benchmark clarification, not in a coded standard description or numbered benchmark. Climate science remains, but some explicit attribution and forecasting tasks are absent.

Why these stand out: Florida’s 2008 adoption included a contested last-minute evolution wording change and explicit climate-change expectations. Later reporting described state climate-language restrictions and textbook edits. That history raises a legitimate question about the new omissions; it does not prove the 2026 workgroup’s motive. Read the sourced history and limits (new tab).

We tested a proposed source connection, too. The 2024 Franklin Standards explicitly name evolution, climate change, Earth’s age, and the universe’s age. Florida’s draft shares broad topic areas but differs in explicit wording and required synthesis; a text screen found no distinctive extended match. The comparison does not establish that Florida adopted or copied Franklin. See the comparison and its limits (new tab).

06 / Public notice and reaction

The review drew competing calls for clarity, rigor, and scrutiny

FDOE opened public review of draft science standards in September 2026, then held listening sessions through October 27. Reporting from those sessions records disagreements about what the benchmarks name explicitly, how much content teachers can cover, and whether the draft gives enough room to question scientific models.

What the state announced

Review period: September–October 2026

The FDOE portal labels the period September–October and lists four listening opportunities, including an October 27 Orange County meeting. The current portal does not give a day-of-month deadline for written comments. On October 29, local reporting said the public still had an opportunity to shape the draft before the State Board’s expected December consideration.

What critics raised

Named expectations and teachability

Teachers and parents questioned whether evolution and human-caused climate change are explicit enough, and whether the volume of standards leaves time for depth. The Florida Education Association called for research-led standards and broader, longer public engagement.

What the department and supporters said

Inquiry, evidence, and critical thinking

Commissioner Henry Mack said climate science and evolution remain in the draft even where familiar terms are not used in each benchmark. Some public commenters supported the revision’s emphasis on questioning models and respectful disagreement. FDOE reported nearly 1,800 comments and revisions in response.

These are selected, attributed statements from public notices and news coverage—not a count of all comments or a representative survey. The comment-period label and meeting schedule are documented; the exact written-comment cutoff date is not stated on the current FDOE portal.

07 / What comes after high school

Florida names college-level science as a goal—show how the draft meets it

FDOE said in its September 25 launch announcement that the new standards should prepare students for college-level science. That is a useful test of the draft: how do its required outcomes prepare students to connect evidence to major scientific explanations? The public-review window began in late September; five weeks without a located university statement is too short to read as assent or as a judgment that no problem exists.

What has precedent

Course review for admission

In the 2008 University of California case, UC’s course-approval process for freshman-admission eligibility included review of some high-school science courses. It was one university system’s review of particular courses, not a Florida standards dispute or an accreditation action.

What remains a risk

Uneven preparation

Students may encounter evidence and mechanisms without being explicitly required to connect them to major explanations such as evolution or human-caused climate change. That could affect readiness, but the standards alone cannot show what schools teach or how universities will respond.

What silence cannot show

The response window is short

As of October 29, the draft had been in public review for about five weeks. We did not locate a university or accreditor statement in that window; this is an early search snapshot, not evidence of assent or lack of concern.

The historical UC case did not involve college-earned credits, several colleges, or Florida’s standards. The current argument is a conditional preparation risk and a request for evidence against FDOE’s stated college-preparation goal—not a prediction that Florida students will be denied admission or credit.

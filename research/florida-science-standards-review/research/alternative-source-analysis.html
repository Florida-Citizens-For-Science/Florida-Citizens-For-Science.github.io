Alternative source comparison: 2026 Florida science draft

Reviewed: October 29, 2026
Stop rule: stop after two alternative source documents with distinctive wording in the draft, or after three hours. The initial review stopped after two document witnesses were identified. At the user’s direction, the comparison continued to test source lineage and additional candidates; the updated findings distinguish source families from derivative witnesses.

Scope and ranking

Three parallel research passes searched the draft against (1) NGSS and federal science-framework materials, (2) Florida-specific alternatives and predecessor records, and (3) state and international standards. A later focused pass tested the Franklin Standards claim by comparing the NAS source directly with the complete Florida grade-band extracts. Candidate ranking considered whether a document predates the draft and was publicly available, whether its source lineage is plausible, and whether a distinctive wording match occurs in the same grade/content context. Common scientific propositions and instructional boilerplate were not counted as lifts. A near-verbatim textual match is evidence of wording lineage, not proof of a particular author’s source or an unauthorized contribution.

Franklin Standards: a comparison source, not a demonstrated source lineage

The NAS and Freedom in Education model standards (2024) were added as a complete source to the private corpus and page-indexed knowledge graph. A normalized eight-word screen compared 44,385 Franklin tokens with 226,964 tokens from Florida’s three grade-band drafts. It found 27 shared sequences; contextual review found only common science formulations such as conservation of energy/mass, spectrum ordering, and Newton’s third law. No distinctive extended wording match was identified.

The topic comparison is useful on its own: Franklin explicitly names biological evolution in elementary and middle grades, dedicates a standard to how and why Earth’s climate changes, states Earth’s age at about 4.6 billion years, and gives an approximate age for the universe alongside Big Bang evidence. Florida retains natural selection, climate mechanisms, human Earth-system effects, radiometric dating, and evidence for cosmic expansion, while placing some named concepts and syntheses differently. Franklin’s own acknowledgments identify Massachusetts 2006 and New York 1996 curricula as inputs. This comparison adds a content contrast, not proof of borrowing. No Florida source note, drafting record, or documentary link to the Franklin project was located. A user-supplied LinkedIn link is listed as a lead only; its inaccessible substantive claims are not used in this analysis. See the topic-by-topic comparison and the bibliography records.

Wording matches that met the stop rule

Florida’s own 2008 predecessor wording

The 2026 draft SC.7.SL.2.1 says populations change through “births, deaths, immigration, emigration, and limiting factors.” Florida’s 2008 SC.912.L.17.5 contains the same seven-word ordered phrase within its population-size benchmark; FDOE’s 2009 science specifications and Biology 1 EOC item specifications reproduce it. The 2026 benchmark moves the idea from high school to grade 7 and recasts the surrounding sentence while retaining the exact ordered list. This is a strong textual-lineage match to Florida’s own earlier benchmark. The phrase is also a conventional population-demography list, so it does not identify whether writers consulted the 2008 PDF, a 2009 item specification, another intervening Florida source, or their own legacy materials.

NGSS Topic Arrangements (2017)

  1. Sun and star brightness: Florida draft SC.2.SP.1.2, Clarification 1 (K–5 PDF p. 96), says the Sun appears brighter than other stars because it is much closer to Earth. NGSS 5-ESS1-1 / ESS1.A (Topic Arrangements PDF p. 31) says the Sun appears larger and brighter than other stars because it is closer. The syntax, comparison, causal explanation, and passage context align closely; the Florida wording drops “larger,” adds “much,” makes the Earth reference explicit, and places it at grade 2 rather than the NGSS grade 5 expectation.

  2. Ecosystem components and populations: Florida draft SC.8.SL.1.3 says to analyze how disruptions to physical or biological components of ecosystems affect populations. NGSS MS-LS2-4 (PDF p. 47) has the corresponding construction: changes to physical or biological components of an ecosystem affect populations. The uncommon phrase sequence and causal claim are retained, with small grammatical and verb changes.

Massachusetts 2016 STE Framework

The Massachusetts framework’s 5-ESS1-1 (official framework PDF p. 51) contains the same Sun-comparison standard as NGSS, including the phrase that the Sun appears larger and brighter than other stars because it is closer to Earth. That language is closely echoed in Florida SC.2.SP.1.2, Clarification 1. Massachusetts is a second plausible source document for the wording, but its standard follows the NGSS formulation. Thus the two source documents are not independent evidence: the immediate source used in Florida, if any, remains unresolved among NGSS, Massachusetts, or an intermediary carrying the same text.

Matches not counted

  • Florida draft SC.6.CB.1.2 states that living organisms are composed of cells. The same seven-word clause appears in the 2007 Florida Citizens for Science proposal and in a California framework sentence that the proposal reproduces. This is standard cell-theory wording, not a distinctive borrowing signal; the Florida document also identifies California as a source.
  • A brief NGSS-related evidence-statement comparison on trait frequencies and environmental change was downgraded: the ideas and some syntax align, but the shared wording is not distinctive enough to count as a lift.
  • The K–5 residual review added conceptual links between SC.K.SL.1.6 / SC.1.SL.1.4 and NGSS 1-LS1-2, which concerns parent/offspring behaviors that help offspring survive. The Florida clauses use a short, conventional expression of this idea, and the benchmarks differ in task framing; this is recorded as concept continuity, not an additional distinctive wording lift.
  • The local 8-token phrase scan of 1996, GLE, 2008, and Florida Citizens for Science materials found only standard energy/system phrases and boilerplate, not another distinctive external-source match.
  • The Franklin comparison found 27 shared eight-word sequences across the 44,385-token source text and 226,964 Florida draft tokens. Contextual review found routine scientific formulations, not a distinctive extended passage. Franklin explicitly names evolution, climate change, Earth’s age, and the universe’s age, making it useful for scope comparison; the overlap does not establish a Florida source relationship. See the comparison note.

Provenance and metadata limits

The Massachusetts PDF’s document properties identify the Massachusetts Department of Elementary and Secondary Education as author and Acrobat PDFMaker as creator. The local copy reports creation on June 2, 2020 and modification on June 15, 2026. These are PDF-file properties, not tracked-change history; they do not establish that the standards wording changed in 2026. The current Florida PDFs likewise expose export/document properties but not the source Word files’ revision history. No claim about external access, authorship of Florida draft wording, or authorization follows from the matches alone.

The Massachusetts Department’s standards page says the current framework was approved in 2016 and received minor revisions in 2020. The Department’s official PDF is used here as the source witness. The NGSS and Massachusetts source files, text extracts, and SHA-256 values are retained under sources/alt-ngss/; Florida/California materials are documented under sources/alt-florida/.

Conclusion

The comparison supports two source families with strong wording lineage in the 2026 draft: (1) Florida’s pre-NGSS 2008 standards, echoed in the grade-shifted population benchmark; and (2) the NRC/NGSS lineage, echoed in the Sun and ecosystem passages. The 2009 Washington framework is a useful independent content comparator for the systems block, but its wording does not meet the lift threshold. Massachusetts is not an independent source family for the Sun passage. This evidence supports likely textual lineage; it does not establish which file or intermediary the workgroup used, who supplied or edited the language, or whether any process access was improper. Draft source files, authoring/version history, and workgroup materials would be needed to answer those questions.

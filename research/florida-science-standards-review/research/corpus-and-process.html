Florida science standards corpus and process comparison

Research updated October 27, 2026. The source files and SHA-256 values are recorded in SHA256SUMS.txt. The five-PDF draft set and its searchable extracts remain the primary benchmark-source witness. The portal’s foreword, tables, forms, and other materials are being tracked separately. See the portal monitoring roadmap and Forms inventory.

Historical corpus assembled

1996 Sunshine State Standards: Science

Four PDFs are now captured for the four original grade clusters: PreK–2, 3–5, 6–8, and 9–12. The copies come from the University of South Florida’s Florida Center for Instructional Technology portal, which preserves the Florida Department of Education standards library. The 6–8 standards PDF was also fetched from an Internet Archive capture of the old FDOE/FIRN URL dated February 3, 2004. Its SHA-256 exactly matches the USF mirror copy. The archived URL is a useful independent chain-of-custody check for that file.

These are scans/exports of the four grade-cluster standards, not the whole Florida Curriculum Framework publication with every possible supporting chapter. They provide the actual standards and benchmarks needed for the main content comparison.

Grade Level Expectations and the “1998” issue

The recovered supporting set contains the science Grade Level Expectations (GLEs) for K, grades 3–5, and grades 6–8, plus the GLE introduction. The grade 6–8 PDF metadata records creation on June 23, 1999 and a later modification in 2002. An Internet Archive capture from October 16, 2004 has the same SHA-256 as the USF copy.

The evidence so far does not support treating “1998 modifications” as a distinct, formally adopted replacement for the 1996 science standards. FDOE’s own history says grade-by-grade expectations were added in 1999. A contemporaneous 1998 review calls its source the “1996 Florida Curriculum Framework – Science” and describes the added GLE material as sample performance descriptions; it says the underlying benchmark statements were unchanged. The GLE files provide the likely intermediate witness: they retain the benchmark identifiers/text while adding grade-level placement and explanations. If “1998 modifications” refers to a different publication, its title or copy is still needed to distinguish it from the 1999 GLEs.

2008 NGSSS Science standards

The 74-page 2008 standards document is captured in sources/2008/. Its provenance is a surviving copy on a non-FDOE domain. The foreword includes the FDOE Science Standards Revision Process narrative, acknowledgments, source materials named by the writers, and the full standards. FDOE independently confirms the February 2008 adoption and points to CPALMS for the governing standards. The local PDF’s front matter is therefore unusually valuable process evidence, while the public file copy itself should be treated as a mirror until compared against the incorporated-by-reference record or a FDOE/CPALMS export.

The foreword says writers used the 2009 NAEP Science Framework, AAAS’s Atlas of Science Literacy and Project 2061 benchmarks, the National Research Council’s National Science Education Standards, and science curriculum standards from Massachusetts, Singapore, South Carolina, and Indiana (K–8). The original Indiana K–8 standards (2000 adoption) are now locally represented by nine ERIC-preserved grade booklets. The official Singapore primary and lower-secondary syllabuses carry 2007 copyright and 2008 implementation dates, and are available online but have reproduction restrictions; the official 1996 NSES reader and Project 2061’s 1993/revised Benchmarks Online are also linked as web controls rather than copied. Exact versions and local/web-only limits are documented in sources/alt-jurisdiction/README.md. The remaining 2005 South Carolina source has not yet been recovered as a local document.

The public FDOE Science Standards Review page (checked October 22, 2026) lists draft benchmark PDFs and an accompanying K–12 tables PDF. The appendix’s “Timeline” is a historical sequence of scientific accomplishments, not a school-year pacing calendar (sources/2026/fl-tables.txt, lines 395–399, 921–925, 1464–1468, 1804–1808). The locally preserved packet contains no semester/week/month map for these draft benchmarks. Therefore, placement near school-year ends or holiday breaks cannot be assessed from code order or PDF order alone; no teaching-time or retention inference is made from document sequence.

FDOE assessment crosswalk between 1996 and 2008

An important intermediate record has now been recovered. FDOE’s March 18, 2008 newsletter says its Assessment Office would prepare a crosswalk from the 1996 science content standards to the newly adopted standards. The department’s March 2010 DPS-2010-50 memo states that similar science crosswalks appear in the 2009 and 2010 FCAT Science Content Focus reports, while cautioning that science standards do not have a one-to-one old-to-new correspondence. The 2009 and 2010 workbooks are now preserved in the crosswalk source folder, with a normalized 276-row alignment table generated by a reproducible extractor.

The workbooks link legacy FCAT benchmark codes to 2008 benchmark alignments. The old codes can be decoded into the 1996 area’s standard, grade cluster, and benchmark number; for example, A123 maps to SC.A.1.2, benchmark 3, in the grades 3–5 source. The 276 rows cover 116 legacy benchmark codes across 49 1996 standard/grade-cluster codes; 247 rows include a 2008 benchmark code and 29 are marked N/A. This is a valuable assessment-level bridge, not a complete equivalence crosswalk for every 1996 standard. It records tested content focus and transition alignment, so absence from these workbooks is not evidence that a standard was deleted. The 2008 workbook is also archived locally but is encrypted and could not be text-extracted. The source folder documents retrieval, code decoding, file metadata, and these limits.

2007 source materials and review record

The preserved FDOE/USF copy of the old Florida Standards portal links a dedicated “Science resources” page explicitly described as the materials used by the framers and writers. I downloaded its four linked framing presentations into sources/2007-process/: AAAS Atlas of Science Literacy, an Achieve presentation, the 2009 NAEP Science Framework, and “Why Do Florida’s K-12 Students Need New Science Standards?” The same page lists National Academies reports, Achieve/NGA, Fordham, TIMSS, PISA, NAEP, Indiana, Massachusetts, South Carolina, Singapore, the National Science Education Standards, and Project 2061. This materially improves the source corpus; the decks are named and dated inputs rather than speculative candidates.

The old portal invited the public to rate benchmarks and comment to the writers. A 2007 Florida Administrative Weekly notice records two rule-development workshops (November 7 and 15) and says online public input began October 19. The FDOE technical assistance paper confirms a crosswalk between old and new standards was planned for the portal. The assessment-level 1996-to-2008 crosswalk described above is now recovered; the public rating/comment records, a complete standards-level crosswalk, meeting notes, and an authoritative version history remain unlocated.

The PowerPoint metadata is also worth preserving. Three downloaded decks list lance.king (or Lance King) as last saver while naming other authors (twillard, Joe Garcia, or mary.tappen) in the Author field; the NAEP deck lists Lance King as both author and last saver. This is compatible with FDOE staff assembling or adapting external expert materials for a workgroup, and is not itself evidence of unauthorized access. The values do establish that the files carry edit-provenance clues worth comparing with source originals and workgroup records. Exact author/title/revision/date metadata and file hashes are in the local files and SHA256SUMS.txt.

Content-level finding: the 1996 return hypothesis is testable

The 1996 text contains explicit evolution-related concepts that matter to the hypothesis:

  • In grades 9–12, SC.F.2.4, Benchmark 3, links mutation and natural selection to adaptation, survival in changing conditions, and species diversity.
  • In grades 9–12, SC.G.2.4, Benchmark 3, links genetic variation among offspring, population/environment, natural selection, adaptation, survival, and reproduction.
  • In grades 6–8, SC.F.2.3, Benchmark 4, says the fossil record shows that the kinds of plants and animals in the environment have changed over time.

The 2026 draft’s middle-school SC.8.SL.2.1–2.4 separates selection conditions, inherited variation, survival/reproduction, population change, and artificial selection. SC.8.SL.3.1–3.2 covers genetic/anatomical/fossil evidence and change over time. This is a strong concept-level correspondence to longstanding Florida content, including the 1996 text. The current wording is not a demonstrated verbatim reuse of the 1996 sentences. The 2008 standards also contain the same core concepts, so concepts alone cannot establish whether a drafting passage came directly from 1996, came through 2008, or was independently written from common science content. A knowledge graph can reveal that lineage while keeping the direct-source claim unresolved.

The 2008 standards use an explicit “Diversity and Evolution of Living Organisms” Big Idea for high school and include natural-selection benchmarks. The 2026 draft relocates and distributes related ideas across the new middle-school population-change/evidence strands and high-school population/genetics standards. This structural movement is exactly where ordered text diffs underperform.

Process comparison and what is public

Documented 2008 process

The 2008 front matter states that FDOE convened framers in May 2007; a writers committee drafted standards from June through October 2007; framers reviewed drafts and returned comments in an iterative process; public online and in-person review ran October 2007 through January 2008; expert reviewers submitted comments and proposed revisions; and the benchmarks were revised in January. It reports 262,524 ratings of 480 standards/benchmarks and 10,017 visitor profiles. The document names the framers, speakers, writers, and expert reviewers. The 2008 standards text is thus accompanied by an explicit process narrative and a participant record.

Documented 2025–2026 process

FDOE’s 2025 planning timeline set a March 2025 introductory call and start of weekly science standards meetings, a March 2026 workgroup draft, and public comment in April 2026. The Florida Administrative Register published a meeting notice with twelve dates between March 18 and April 29, 2025, including Teams links; it says agendas could be obtained from the FDOE contact. FDOE’s draft was instead launched for public comment on September 25, 2026, about five months after the planned April window.

The live FDOE Standards Review portal (checked October 27, 2026) identifies the science public-comment period as September–October 2026, labels the three grade-band PDFs as review-only/non-final, and lists listening-tour sessions on September 29–30, October 5, and October 27. The captured HTML contains 35 Forms anchor placements and 26 unique online form URLs, alongside three grade-band standards PDFs and separate foreword/tables materials. The K–5 Part 3 form URL appears under six labels, including one labeled “K–5 Part 4”; the link-label/target mismatch is preserved for review. The maintained link inventory preserves heading context and classifies the proposed grade-band standards separately from supporting PDFs, review forms, and older standards-review records elsewhere on the page. All five active PDFs were fetched in the October 27 dated snapshot and SHA-256 matched against the existing corpus. Across dated captures and targeted retries, all 26 Forms have visible text; 25 reached the final page without submission and one K–5 Part 3 capture remains partial at the 60-page limit. The first reconciliation identified 11 separate K–12 STARS standards absent from the three grade-band PDFs and the 1,512-row leaf-benchmark register; these remain outside the benchmark denominator pending taxonomy review. Full portal/form source text is preserved in the private local corpus. The October 1, 2026 rule-development notice says the proposed amendment would consider the newly revised science standards identified during review, and lists no reference materials. These records document public-review logistics and the agency’s rulemaking step; they do not expose the working group’s draft history, comment dispositions, or who supplied individual benchmark language. The monitoring roadmap records the capture plan and the unresolved exact comment close date.

This corrects the idea that no public record of FDOE/workgroup interaction exists: meeting schedules and a weekly-meeting plan are public. In the public records reviewed so far, however, I have not found the meeting agendas/minutes, version-by-version drafts, change logs, written FDOE feedback, or the response matrix that would show how iteration happened. The scheduled meetings prove opportunities and a planned cadence, not the content or completeness of the interactions. The schedule slip is a timeline fact, not evidence that the process was shortcut.

The 2024 FDOE call for workgroup members describes the intended participation criteria. Contemporary reporting describes the selected committee as 18 members, mostly district employees with one science-education professor. A primary FDOE roster/selection record has not yet been located. The 2025 notice offers an obvious request route for agendas, but no records request has been sent.

FDOE’s September 25, 2026 press release launching the listening tour adds relevant evidence about the administration’s public framing. Commissioner Henry Mack’s quotation invokes a classical tradition in which “the book of creation can be read by reason”; the release also calls for distinguishing established knowledge from “political fashion” and for classrooms to cultivate virtue. This rhetoric warrants preserving as context for the sectarian-shading inquiry, but it is not a standards text or workgroup record. It does not establish that religious content entered the draft or that the workgroup shared or acted on the press-release framing. A source note with scope limits is preserved in sources/2026-process/.

Adoption boundary and material layers

Interpret the review packet in layers. Florida Statutes §1003.41 says new and revised standards documents submitted to the State Board must consist only of academic standards and benchmarks; the current Rule 6A-1.09401 still references the 2016 science standards, and the 2026 PDFs remain review-only/non-final. The coded parent descriptions and numbered benchmark statements are therefore the primary layer for this review’s claims about explicit required content. Clarifications, examples, student tasks, the foreword/tables, and form-only STARS statements are tracked separately. Their eventual legal effect cannot be settled by their location on the portal alone: some FDOE standards in other subjects explicitly adopt clarifications/examples, so the final science rule’s exact incorporated reference and document version must be checked. See the full source-layer, word-count, and legal-status analysis and the primary-source bibliography.

Generative-AI authorship and process evidence

The efficiency or polished uniformity of a standards packet is not evidence that generative AI produced it. Standards use repeated templates and coordinated editing, both of which can create those same surface features. The PDF metadata reviewed here identifies FDOE-affiliated document handlers/exporters and ordinary PDF production; the two GrammarlyDocumentId markers show that the documents passed through Grammarly at some point, not that a generative feature was used. None of these observations confirms or excludes AI-assisted drafting.

For this case, the most probative evidence would be process records rather than a prose classifier:

  1. Obtain original Word files, including DOCX/DOCM properties, comments, tracked revisions, embedded objects, and retained prior versions. Preserve the native files before conversion and compare all dated drafts, not just final PDFs.
  2. Request SharePoint/OneDrive/Office version history and document-management audit logs, including creation, editing, downloading, sharing, and export events; identify which accounts and devices made large insertions or rewrites. The records available depend on the actual storage system and retention configuration.
  3. Request FDOE and contractor policies, procurement and subscription records, product/tenant access logs, and any retained prompts, generated outputs, or AI interaction history for Microsoft Copilot or other generative systems. Ask who had access and whether logs were retained; absent logs do not prove a tool was or was not used. If FDOE used Microsoft 365, ask specifically whether Purview AI-interaction auditing was enabled and request Copilot/AI-app audit exports and eDiscovery records. Microsoft’s current documentation says eligible Copilot/AI-app interaction records can include the prompt, response, user, app identity, time, and accessed files; third-party AI-app interaction logs require a pay-as-you-go feature and are retained for 180 days when enabled. These are conditional leads, not evidence FDOE used those tools or enabled those records (Microsoft Purview audit guidance).
  4. Compare version-to-version text and independently search distinctive passages against public standards and source documents. A matched source can establish textual lineage even when it cannot say whether a person or model selected or paraphrased it.

Commercial or open text detectors can be used only as low-weight triage. Research evaluations have found poor out-of-domain sensitivity at low false-positive rates, while prompting or paraphrasing can evade detectors; one study reports true-positive rates as low as zero at a 1% false-positive rate in some tested settings. Results should be validated on a held-out set of known human-authored standards, known model outputs, and mixed human/AI examples of similar length and genre. A detector score cannot identify the model, the user, the prompt, the amount of assistance, or whether the use was authorized. See Tufts et al. (2024), Kumarage et al. (2023), and Krishna et al. (2023).

The public PDF packet is insufficient to determine generative-AI use. The original editable files, version history, workgroup records, and any relevant tool/tenant records are still needed. This review treats AI use as an open records question, not as an inference from speed, style, or Grammarly metadata.

Knowledge-graph comparison method

knowledge-graph-schema.json defines versioned documents, textual units, concepts, relations, and evidence assertions. concept-graph-seed.json instantiates the evolution/natural-selection crosswalk across 1996, 1999 GLE, 2008, and 2026. The graph preserves each original benchmark ID and its text, then connects it to one or more concepts and to the source assertion that supports the relationship. This lets matching survive grade shifts, reorganized strand codes, splits/merges, paraphrase, and wording changes.

The graph should store at least these layers separately:

  1. Text witness: exact text, code, grade span, PDF page, document hash, and retrieval source.
  2. Concept node: normalized scientific idea, with parent/child concepts (e.g. inherited variation → population trait frequency → differential survival/reproduction → population change over generations).
  3. Curriculum relation: a benchmark teaches, exemplifies, assesses, presupposes, or places a concept in a grade/course.
  4. Cross-version relation: retained, revised, relocated, split/merged, removed, or introduced; allow multiple candidate predecessors.
  5. Provenance evidence: direct citation, exact-phrase match, conceptual match, meeting/comment/draft record, confidence, and an explicit “source unknown” state.

Do not collapse “same scientific concept” into “copied from this source.” The latter needs a source-bearing record such as an identified drafting input, writer note, change history, or distinctive textual match. Concept graph edges are evidence of curricular continuity; textual and process records are needed for authorship and source attribution.

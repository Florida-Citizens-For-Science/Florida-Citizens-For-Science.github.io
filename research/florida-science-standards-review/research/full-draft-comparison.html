2026 Florida science draft: content, sequence, omissions, and provenance

Forms reconciliation has expanded the review scope

The 1,512-row register and the 1,468/44 coverage figures describe the three grade-band PDFs. Dated captures now cover all 26 online forms (25 complete, one partial) and identify 11 numbered K–12 STARS standards absent from those PDFs and the current register. These are a separate standard set, not additional grade-band leaf benchmarks; their detailed analysis remains distinct from PDF-based findings.

See the monitoring and reconciliation roadmap.

Historical context makes these headline omissions

In 2008, evolution wording was contested at Florida’s standards vote; the Board adopted evolution with a late “scientific theory of” qualifier. The adopted standards also explicitly named global climate change. Later reporting described state-agency climate-term restrictions under Rick Scott and climate-content revisions requested for textbooks under Ron DeSantis. In the 2026 draft’s coded standard descriptions and numbered benchmarks, biological evolution and common ancestry are not named; “climate change” is absent as a coded learning expectation and appears once in a benchmark clarification. The records establish history and context, not the 2026 workgroup’s motive.

Executive findings

  • The 2026 draft retains substantial instruction about natural selection, fossils, climate systems, and human effects on Earth. No numbered benchmark uses “evolution” biologically; the only parent-standard use is the geological phrase “landscape evolution” (SC.912.EE.4). “Climate change” appears once in a benchmark clarification, not in coded standard or benchmark text. These findings concern explicit wording and requirements; related concepts remain elsewhere in the draft.
  • The 2008 standards include high-school expectations about human ancestry and fossil hominids; no equivalent 2026 benchmark was located. The 2008 grade-7 standards ask how scientists measure Earth’s age, while the 2026 draft moves rock-dating tasks to high school without explicitly asking students to determine Earth’s age. The 2008 high-school benchmark names the Big Bang as the universe’s origin; the 2026 draft retains evidence for expansion and cosmic background radiation but names neither the Big Bang nor the universe’s age.
  • Leaf-benchmark wording alone totals about 16,403 words in 2026 versus 15,675 in 2008 (+4.6%). Including coded parent-standard descriptions on both sides gives about 23,712 versus 18,875 words (+25.6%). This is a broader comparison of coded standards-and-benchmarks text, though the frameworks differ in structure and word totals do not equal teachable targets or workload. The 2026 grade-band PDFs contain 216,632 extracted words because they also include clarifications, examples, student tasks, instructional notes, headings, and layout text; the foreword and tables add 23,039. Those are packet totals, not standards-text totals. Count data: JSON. See the scope and counting method.
  • The 2026 draft does not name common descent/common ancestry as the unifying scientific account of life’s relationships. Coded statements retain natural selection, population variation/change, and fossil evidence. Some clarifications discuss evolutionary and phylogenetic relationships, but they are not stated in the coded parent or leaf text. The 2008 standards did not use the exact “common descent” phrase either, but explicitly called evolution the organizing principle of life science and said it was supported by multiple forms of evidence. The 2026 draft’s omission of that synthesis is a substantive, sectarianly consequential divergence aligned with antievolution opposition to shared ancestry; it is not by itself proof of the drafters’ intent. See the TalkOrigins claim-context review.
  • The clearest grade relocation in the reviewed high-interest areas is the middle-school natural-selection cluster: 2008 grade 7 SC.7.L.15.1–.3 becomes grade 8 SC.8.SL.2.1–.4. Fossil evidence also appears in grade 8. The 2026 high-school evolution material is reorganized and no longer uses the 2008 high-school “Diversity and Evolution” organizing label.
  • Climate is reorganized from 2008 standards that explicitly name global climate change and ask students to relate internal and astronomical conditions to it. The 2026 draft gives more detailed attention to radiation balance, greenhouse processes, circulation, and feedback, and separately addresses anthropogenic Earth-system interactions. The register now links the draft human-impact sequence to 2008 Earth-system, climate-change, biodiversity-loss, biogeochemical-cycle, and sustainability benchmarks while recording its added modeling and evidence-evaluation demands. The specific 2008 external-astronomical-forcing task and the NGSS century-scale attribution and climate-model forecasting tasks are not explicit in the located 2026 benchmark statements; these are partial or possible omissions, not evidence that climate change is absent.
  • The climate comparison also finds narrower task and placement differences: the 2008 grade-5 climate-zone task is retained in grade 4 and extended by grade-5 biome comparisons; elevation and proximity-to-water factors are treated separately. NGSS elementary design/build and hazard-solution tasks are represented mainly as observation/preparation at elementary grades and mitigation evaluation in high school; probabilistic weather forecasting is not explicit. NGSS large-scale circulation is present in Florida’s high-school draft sequence rather than at middle school.
  • Why the omissions matter: in evolution, the missing explicit synthesis of shared ancestry and the omission of human-evolution expectations risk leaving evidence and mechanisms disconnected from the explanation they support. In climate, the draft does ask students to evaluate evidence about human interactions with atmospheric and energy processes, but does not clearly require the century-scale attribution and climate-model forecasting tasks included in national standards. If aligned curriculum and assessment do not fill these gaps, Florida students may have to assemble core explanations that other frameworks state directly, with instruction depending more heavily on local supplementation. This is a risk to coherent, accurate teaching and equitable access, not proof that every classroom will teach inaccurately. See the instructional-integrity analysis. FDOE-authored clarifications and examples may carry greater perceived authority than an independent supplement, making added synthesis harder for districts to adopt or teachers to use. That is a plausible implementation risk, not an outcome this review has measured. Any supplement should map visibly to the official benchmark, distinguish FDOE wording from added instruction, and be checked with teachers and district curriculum staff before broader investment.
  • A full scan of the five public draft files found no explicit sectarian insertion, creationist content, anti-expert claims, or climate/evolution denialist rhetoric. A separate FDOE press release launching the review uses religiously resonant “book of creation” language and calls for cultivating virtue; that is relevant process context, not evidence of an insertion into the standards or of workgroup intent. Within the draft, conventional uncertainty and model-limit language is paired with evidence evaluation, independent sources, peer review, revision, and consensus. A newly verified K–5 scope divergence concerns conductors: Florida 2008’s grade-4 thermal-conduction concept is retained in grade 3, while the distinct grade-5 electrical-conductivity task is explicitly excluded by the draft clarification.
  • The register lists all 1,512 unique 2026 leaf benchmark IDs, and every row has been through candidate review. Current manually classified comparisons cover 1,468 rows: 41 divergences, 3 feature-specific non-divergent comparisons, 355 high-confidence concept matches, and 1,069 medium-confidence concept matches. The other 44 remain unresolved after review against the available corpus. Concept-match labels establish continuity of an idea, but do not by themselves classify all dimensions as non-divergent. Unresolved means the available comparison did not support a reliable verdict; it does not mean a benchmark is new or unchanged.
  • The alternative-source review found two outside document witnesses with distinctive near-verbatim wording in the draft, NGSS Topic Arrangements and the Massachusetts 2016 framework. They belong to one NGSS-related source family, so they are not two independent pathways. Florida’s own 2008 standards also share a distinctive ordered phrase in a grade-shifted population benchmark. None of these matches identifies who supplied the wording or proves unauthorized access.
  • The public 2026 PDFs do not preserve source DOCX files or tracked-change histories. PDF author fields and two Grammarly document identifiers identify export or processing traces, not authorship or unauthorized access. Workgroup schedule records are public; version-by-version drafts, minutes, comments, and change logs remain absent from the reviewed corpus.

Terminology, supporting text, and learning progression

A lexical audit of the three 2026 grade-band files found no “evolution,” “climate change,” or “global warming” in the 1,512 numbered benchmark statements. The coded parent descriptions include one use of “evolution” in the geological phrase “landscape evolution” (SC.912.EE.4), but no biological-evolution or evolutionary wording. In the full grade-band files, “climate change” occurs once in a benchmark clarification listing human impacts on mammals. “Human evolution,” “hominid,” “common descent,” and “common ancestry” do not appear in coded parent or leaf statements. The 2008 benchmark texts use “evolution” in 10 rows and “climate change” in three; they include one human-evolution benchmark and one about hominid fossils. These are lexical findings, not a substitute for concept comparison.

Word-count comparison, with scope shown: leaf-benchmark wording alone totals about 16,403 words in 2026 and 15,675 in 2008 (+4.6%). Parent-standard descriptions add 7,309 words across 336 coded 2026 parent standards and 3,200 words across 169 lettered 2008 parent clauses. Combined parent descriptions and leaf benchmarks total approximately 23,712 words in 2026 and 18,875 in 2008 (+25.6%). The registers contain 1,512 versus 498 leaf items. Because the frameworks divide and bundle expectations differently, neither item count nor word total directly measures teachable targets or student workload. See the full method and adoption-status discussion.

The much larger 2026 PDF totals measure a different thing. The three grade-band PDFs total 987 pages and 216,632 extracted words, including benchmark text plus embedded clarifications, examples, student tasks, and instructional notes. About 200,200 extracted words fall outside the numbered benchmark statements; that remainder also includes headings, repeated page labels, and other layout text. The separate foreword (6 pages/1,351 words) and K–12 tables (91 pages/21,688 words) bring the five-PDF review packet to 1,084 pages and 239,671 extracted words. These supporting sections are part of the official material teachers and reviewers may consult, but they are not additional numbered benchmark statements and do not amount to a complete curriculum. The 2008 PDF’s 74 pages/24,217 extracted words are not an apples-to-apples comparator for this more elaborated packet. Page and extracted-word counts do not measure teaching time or educational quality; the corpus has no statewide pacing calendar.

The additional material is benchmark-linked instructional guidance: clarifications, examples, and sample tasks can shape how a benchmark is interpreted and taught. Its position in the review packet does not settle its eventual legal status; the final rule’s incorporated document controls. The PDFs do not provide full lesson sequences, a school-year pacing map, or a complete set of teacher and student materials. Some sample tasks may nevertheless create real preparation, materials, and assessment demands. Those should be assessed directly rather than inferred from page or word totals.

Instructional-time claims need a separate test. A recent article by science-education researcher Dr. Victor Sampson highlights the proposed foreword’s statement that benchmarks, clarifications, and examples together carry the standards’ full weight, then estimates the time needed to teach the high-school material. The foreword supports treating these items as part of the draft’s intended instructional expectations; it also says related benchmarks should be integrated rather than taught as isolated units. The article’s clarification count and time ratios are useful prompts for scrutiny, but the “24 credits to introduce” and “73 to master” results depend on treating each clarification as one topic, assigning a fixed number of class periods to every topic, and assuming all 9–12 expectations fall within each student’s three required science credits. Florida Statutes §1003.4282 establishes the three-credit graduation minimum; §1003.436 defines a credit as at least 135 instructional hours (120 under authorized block scheduling). Neither provision maps every grade-band benchmark to those courses. No statewide course-to-benchmark map or pacing evidence was available for this review. We therefore treat the article as a scenario analysis that motivates direct workload measurement, not proof that the draft cannot be taught in the available time. See the source note and source-layer explanation.

The current knowledge graph links benchmark text to concepts and possible predecessors, but it does not contain a reviewed prerequisite graph. Its schema allows requiresConcept edges; the published graph has seven concept nodes and no prerequisite edges. Didactopus is relevant because its graph tools can analyze dependency structures, but its current graph-QA checks focus on cycles, isolated concepts, dependency concentration, sparse graphs, and deep chains; they do not directly compare prerequisite order with the Florida benchmark order. The Didactopus repository and its graph-analysis documentation were inspected for this review, but the analyzer was not run because the current standards graph has no reviewed prerequisite edges for it to assess. That comparison needs educator-reviewed dependency edges and a reliable intended sequence. The 2026 packet assigns grade levels but gives no week-by-week or semester pacing. PDF/code order alone cannot show when teachers present a topic. This report does not yet claim that the draft violates a coherent learning progression. The proposed learnability-order and pedagogical-burden method explains how to build and validate a partial-order graph, assess only sequencing cues the documents actually provide, and estimate instructional burden without converting broad time/achievement correlations into unsupported hours-to-mastery claims.

Scope and method

The omission analysis compares Florida 2008 and NGSS separately, then checks additional external frameworks. The omission crosswalk summarizes candidate concepts/tasks not explicit in the 2026 benchmark text. The three initially unanchored-source reviews, grades 6–8, and grades 9–12 cover 171 IDs that lacked register links before code normalization; the three direct-anchor reviews for K–5, grades 6–8, and grades 9–12 assess all 328 directly cited 2008 IDs for task equivalence. Across all 498 unique 2008 benchmark IDs, 35 receive a possible-omission disposition; a complete inventory and qualifications are in the crosswalk. The crosswalk screens all 208 NGSS Performance Expectations, with 12 representative human-reviewed comparisons, and separately identifies further-external-framework concepts not explicit or less explicit in the draft. These are benchmark-text findings; an absent explicit benchmark does not establish that teachers cannot address the concept. The surviving 1996 standards and K–8 1998/99 GLEs were also reviewed row by row across all 196 source IDs (114 retained/relocated, 75 partial, 7 possible omissions, 0 unresolved); six possible-omission concept clusters and full row locators are summarized in the crosswalk.

The reviewed 2026 packet is the five-file Florida Department of Education review set: K–5, grades 6–8, grades 9–12, foreword, and supplemental K–12 tables. Text extracts are retained alongside the PDFs under sources/2026/. The register was audited with whitespace-tolerant code parsing after 11 leaf IDs with embedded spaces were discovered and added. The corrected coverage is 1,512 unique IDs: 279 K–5, 236 grades 6–8, and 997 grades 9–12. The full text, including clarifications, examples, tasks, and tables, was also included in the rhetoric review and searchable corpus.

Historical comparison materials include Florida’s 1996 standards, surviving 1998/99 grade-level expectations (GLEs), adopted 2008 standards, 2007 process materials, and the 2024 workgroup call. The broader external-source corpus includes NGSS/NRC materials, California 2006, Massachusetts 2006 and 2016, South Carolina 2014, nine Indiana K–8 grade booklets adopted in 2000–01, a Washington 2009 comparison inspected through an indexed copy, and the 2007 Florida Citizens for Science proposal. The contemporaneous Singapore 2008 primary and lower-secondary syllabuses, the official 1996 NSES reader, and AAAS Project 2061’s original/revised Benchmarks Online are documented as web-accessible comparison controls but not copied locally because of source reproduction restrictions or version limits. The preserved 1998/99 GLEs do not include a high-school GLE text; they are grade-level elaborations, not evidence of a formally adopted replacement to the 1996 standards.

Dedicated residual audits cover the high-school animal, fungi, microorganism, matter, and motion strands: animal biology, fungi, microorganisms, matter, and motion. The animal audit began with 97 unresolved rows; after subsequent source reviews, the current register has 13 unresolved animal benchmarks. The fungi strand has one unresolved row, and the microorganism strand has none; five unresolved human-microbiome benchmarks remain in human biology. Follow-up checks linked fungal reproduction benchmarks to Florida 1996, 1998/99 GLE, and 2008 concepts and a microbial primary-producer benchmark to NGSS LS1.C; each link preserves the draft’s added specificity and makes no provenance claim. Current counts come from the live register; the residual audits preserve historical checkpoints as well as row-level evidence. These corpus-bounded findings do not treat source absence as proof of omission.

The benchmark register and candidate retrieval CSVs are discovery and tracking tools. A similarity score is not a match verdict. Each confirmed comparison requires reading the cited predecessor text in context. Concept continuity does not demonstrate wording provenance. The exact wording and process evidence are assessed separately in alternative-source-analysis.md and corpus-and-process.md.

Grade placement and curricular sequence

Evolution and population change

The 2008 standards place fossil/evolution evidence and natural selection in grade 7 (SC.7.L.15.1–.3, printed pp.48–49) and repeat a detailed evolution sequence in grades 9–12 (SC.912.L.15.1–.15, printed pp.68–70). The 1996 standards cover adaptation/fossil change in the 6–8 band and natural-selection mechanisms in high school; the 1998/99 GLE narrows some placements, putting fossil evidence and adaptations in grade 8.

The 2026 draft places the explicit middle-school selection sequence in grade 8 (SC.8.SL.2.1–.4, draft pp.136–140), followed by evidence and fossil-record benchmarks (SC.8.SL.3.1–.3.3, pp.141–142). Grade 5 includes an expectation that advantageous traits may become more common, while several K–5 clarifications exclude advanced or detailed evolutionary mechanisms. These K–5 exclusions are narrow scope statements; they do not erase the explicit grade 8 or high-school content.

Grades 9–12 retain variation, survival, reproductive success, and population change in SC.912.SL.6.1–.6 (pp.199–203). However, the 2008 high-school details for molecular clocks, reproductive isolation/speciation, Hardy–Weinberg calculations, hominid evolution, genetic drift, and gene flow were not located as explicit 2026 benchmark tasks. The broader source-first review also flags possible gaps in science/nonscience boundaries, pseudoscience, general theory/law distinctions, selected physics and anatomy outcomes, the near-universality of the genetic code (SC.912.L.16.9), forensic DNA identification/RFLP (.16.11), and the DNA-cloning workflow (.16.12). These are candidate specificity or application gaps documented in the omission crosswalk and its grade-band appendices, not evidence that evolution, genetics, or biotechnology as a whole is missing.

The six high-school population-change rows are marked divergent for their task emphasis and organization, not because evolution lacks a predecessor. Their updated register notes now identify continuity with 2008/1996 mutation, natural-selection, survival, and reproductive-success concepts, while distinguishing the draft’s population measurement, multi-generation data, applied examples, and evaluation tasks.

These omissions matter for the coherence and accuracy of what students are expected to learn. The National Academies’ K–12 framework treats evolution as “unity and diversity,” including shared ancestry and converging evidence; it also describes natural selection and speciation as parts of that explanation. The 2026 draft retains important evidence and mechanisms, but the located benchmarks do not explicitly require students to synthesize them through a common-descent explanation, apply evolutionary reasoning to human ancestry, or study several mechanisms and evidence tools named in 2008. The likely effect is not established classroom failure; it is that the standards leave more of the explanatory synthesis to supplemental curriculum, assessment, and individual teachers. National Academies, Framework, Chapter 6: Biological Evolution.

The literal phrase “births, deaths, immigration, emigration, and limiting factors” in 2026 SC.7.SL.2.1 also appears in 2008 SC.912.L.17.5. This is a strong wording-lineage match and a grade shift from high school to grade 7. Because the list is conventional population-demography language, the immediate drafting source could have been the 2008 standards, a later Florida item specification, or another intermediary.

Climate and Earth systems

The 2008 standards teach grade 6 weather/climate fundamentals (SC.6.E.7.2–.6, printed p.43). High-school SC.912.E.7.6–.7 (printed p.60) explicitly asks about the ocean’s effect on climate change and the internal Earth-system and external astronomical conditions contributing to global climate change. The 1996 standards discuss long-term changes in climate and Earth systems and identify carbon dioxide released by burning fossil fuels; they do not, in the cited passage, directly connect that emission to contemporary global climate change.

The 2026 draft distributes climate learning across grade 6 (SC.6.PE.1.2–.3), grade 8 (SC.8.PE.1.1–.5 and .2.1–.2), and high-school SC.912.PE.13.1–.6 (radiation balance, greenhouse processes, circulation, ocean heat storage, feedback, synthesis) and .PE.16.1–.7 (anthropogenic Earth-system interactions, evidence, and sustainability). The 2026 scope is substantial. The climate-cluster wording does not directly reproduce the 2008 task linking astronomical forcing to global climate change or the NGSS task combining century-scale global temperature rise with the major role of human activity. Ocean heat and water movement are present; the explicit ocean–carbon–climate linkage is less direct.

This supports a finding of reframing and redistribution, with a candidate omission of a few specific tasks. It does not support a finding that climate instruction has been removed or that uncertainty language is being used to discredit climate research.

The distinction between retained topic coverage and missing synthesis matters for accurate instruction. The draft teaches greenhouse mechanisms, climate regulation, data analysis, and human effects on Earth systems; SC.912.PE.16.2 explicitly asks students to evaluate evidence about anthropogenic interactions with atmospheric and energy-related processes. What is not explicit in the located benchmark text is a task asking students to use evidence to identify the factors that have caused climate change over the past century, as NGSS MS-ESS3-5 does, or to use climate-model outputs to forecast rates and impacts, as HS-ESS3-5 does. Nor does the draft explicitly connect its solar-activity item to climate attribution or restore the 2008 ocean–carbon–climate task. The U.S. Global Change Research Program’s 2024 Climate Literacy Guide treats observations, modeling, natural drivers, and human-caused recent change as connected parts of climate literacy. Without an explicit synthesis task, students could encounter accurate component facts without being required to use them to explain observed change. That is a risk to curricular coherence, not evidence that teachers cannot make the connection or that the draft denies human-caused warming.

The expanded NGSS screen identifies additional grade-specific task differences: the draft kindergarten sunlight/shade benchmark observes and models warming but does not include the NGSS design/build challenge to reduce warming; probabilistic weather forecasting is not explicit; and the grade-3 hazard-design merit task is not stated at that grade. The draft does include grade-8 community hazard data, risk assessment, and mitigation recommendations (SC.8.PE.4.1), aligning with the purpose of NGSS MS-ESS3-2; it does not state the same contrast between hazards that can be forecast and those that remain unpredictable. Climate-zone and biome comparisons are present in grades 4–5. Large-scale atmospheric circulation is present in the Florida draft but the detailed rotation-driven circulation-cell treatment is located in high school, versus NGSS middle school. The omission crosswalk records each comparison and its limits.

Universe expansion

The grade-8 draft explicitly says the universe is expanding in SC.8.SP.2.3 and models distant galaxies moving farther apart. The 2008 standards describe galaxy-scale structure (SC.8.E.5.2–.3) and ask students to evaluate evidence for Big Bang theory at high school (SC.912.E.5.1); the surviving 1996/GLE passages describe the universe’s vastness and distant stars. None of those located benchmark statements explicitly names expansion. The register therefore records a divergence in explicit benchmark-level scope, while noting that the 2008 Big Bang evidence task could have allowed related instruction. This does not establish that the idea was wholly absent from prior teaching or identify the draft’s source.

Other documented grade shifts

The manual crosswalk also finds concept matches involving renewable/nonrenewable resources, Moon phases, cell theory, ecosystems, heredity, atomic structure, star life cycles, and Solar System placement. Additional reviewed shifts include atom structure from grade 8 in 2008 to grade 6 in the draft; physical/chemical property and change distinctions from grade 8 to grade 7; macromolecule structure/function from high school to grade 8; distance-time graph interpretation from grade 6 to grade 7; circuit-energy concepts from grade 5 to grade 8; and mass-dependent star evolution from high school in the 1996/2008 standards and grade 7 in the GLE to grade 6 in the draft. Space-exploration content formerly repeated at grades 4, 8, and high school is recast as a high-school evaluation of technology benefits and challenges, adding safety, energy, environmental, workforce, and community-growth dimensions. These are concept-level links with task and scope differences recorded in the register. One example: the GLEs placed an explanation of lunar phases in grades 3–5; 2008 also includes Moon phases in grade 8; the 2026 draft gives elementary students recognition of repeating Moon-phase patterns and reserves the causes/individual naming for grade 8. These comparisons describe grade placement, not within-year pacing.

Within-year sequencing and calendar placement: The public review packet does not provide a week-, month-, semester-, or quarter-based pacing guide. FDOE’s Science Standards Review page (checked October 22, 2026) supplies the draft benchmark PDFs, a foreword, and a K–12 tables PDF. Although that PDF’s title includes “Timeline,” its tables are titled “Timeline of Key Accomplishments Supporting Mastery” and organize historical discoveries by time period, accomplishment, relevance, and people (for example, sources/2026/fl-tables.txt, lines 395–399 and 921–925); it is not an instructional calendar. The published document order and benchmark codes support comparisons of grade placement and topical organization only. They cannot establish whether any concept is planned for the end of a school year, immediately before a holiday, or in a sequence intended to affect retention. No scheduling motive or learning-time effect is inferred without an actual district/course pacing map tied to this draft.

Historical-source anchors recorded in the benchmark register

These sets overlap; 1,290 distinct draft benchmarks have at least one such Florida historical locator. The counts are register-anchor presence, not coverage or equivalence scores: a draft benchmark may be supported by more than one source, and an anchor establishes only the concept or feature described in its note.

The draft-first unresolved screen preserves the top-five retrieval results for the original 135-row review queue across 902 source records. After adjudication of supported recommendations and four additional GLE predecessor links, 44 rows remain unresolved in the current register; all 135 queue rows have source-first review in the linked K–8, animal/human biology, fungi/microbes, or remaining high-school appendix. The reviews distinguish partial concept matches, scope divergences, and unsupported comparisons. Similarity rank remains a navigation aid, not evidence by itself.

Some concept-match rows cite external comparison material, including NGSS migration expectations, California animal-feature material, and NGSS Earth-interior and natural-hazard expectations. Such links are explicitly marked as content comparators; they are not evidence that the Florida working group accessed or borrowed the cited source. Six high-school evolution divergence rows cite 2008/1996 locators for their documented task and data differences. The remaining divergence and feature-specific non-divergence labels remain distinct. The 1996 scans and surviving GLEs are incomplete, and no high-school GLE text is present in the corpus; a missing locator in those versions cannot establish that a concept was historically absent.

The larger high-school unresolved count reflects both the size and specialization of that benchmark set; it does not indicate more change or less change by itself. “Concept match” records a supported predecessor concept, not full equivalence across wording, grade, sequence, scope, and pedagogy. The three non-divergent statuses concern one stated feature per row, not global equivalence.

The remaining 44 register-unresolved rows are 1 in K–5, 6 in grades 6–8, and 37 in grades 9–12. The high-school set includes 13 animal-science rows, nine cellular-biology rows, five human-biology/microbiome rows, three plant-science rows, two energy rows, two applied-life-science rows, and one each in fungi, force/motion, and nutrition. All six middle-school unresolved rows concern nutrition. The categories follow benchmark-code families and are not measures of change. Their row-level reviews explain where partial comparisons or scope differences were found and where the corpus still cannot resolve lineage. The register label unresolved does not mean new or omitted. The K–5 food-supply-chain task is the sole register-unresolved elementary row. The focused review files are linked in the omission appendix. A reverse review of the 2008 conductors standards records both the grade-4 thermal-conduction carryover and the grade-5 electrical-conductivity task explicitly excluded from the grade-3 benchmark. The external-source and source-first screens remain candidate-finding tools, not substitutes for checking each cited passage.

Only three rows receive a feature-specific non-divergent finding: SC.8.SL.3.1–.3, where the fossil/change-evidence concept persists at grade 8, consistent with the located 1998/99 GLE placement. Seven former high-school climate-system labels were returned to unresolved because their notes lacked an established benchmark-level predecessor; that correction is reflected in the current counts.

Within-year pacing and holiday timing

The documents reviewed specify grade-level expectations and arrange them in PDF order; they do not supply a statewide quarter-by-quarter scope and sequence, a pacing calendar, holiday alignment, or standards-to-assessment calendar. Therefore, the hypothesis that a benchmark’s position near a break or at year-end reduces instructional time or recall cannot be tested from these documents. A district scope-and-sequence, course map, teacher guide, assessment blueprint, and the local school calendar would be needed. PDF/page order is not evidence of the intended order within a school year.

Concepts in 2008/NGSS and other standards that may not be explicit in 2026

The candidate omission crosswalk separates three questions: which concepts/tasks in the 2008 Florida standards are absent or less explicit in 2026; which NGSS performance expectations are absent, less explicit, retained, or relocated; and what other preserved external standards/frameworks include that is absent or less explicit in 2026. The machine screen covers all 498 unique 2008 benchmark IDs and all 208 NGSS performance expectations in the preserved AllTopic document; human review remains selective, not a complete adjudication of every source standard against every 2026 benchmark. Further external comparisons include California 2006, Indiana K–8 (412 source statements machine-screened, with human review incomplete), Massachusetts 2006/2016, South Carolina 2014, the Washington 2009 systems sequence, and Florida Citizens for Science 2007. The report distinguishes specific task/mechanism gaps from broad subject coverage and does not treat an external comparator as evidence of access or borrowing.

The detailed omission crosswalk separates adopted Florida 2008 standards, NGSS performance expectations, and further external comparison documents. The following summary distinguishes specific content/task gaps from broad subject coverage; each item is provisional unless its row says otherwise.

Florida 2008 concepts less explicit or not located in 2026

The source-first review assigns dispositions to all 498 unique 2008 benchmark IDs: 234 retained or relocated, 227 partial, 35 possible omissions, and 2 unresolved. “Possible omission” applies to a particular competency or task that was not located in the reviewed draft packet; it does not mean the broader subject disappeared from instruction. The full 35-ID inventory, grouped by grade band with source wording and row-level rationale, is in the 2008 disposition table. The examples below highlight several especially relevant content and task differences.

  • 2008 Florida: explicit astronomical conditions linked to global climate change (SC.912.E.7.7); integrated ocean heat/carbon/water effects on climate change (SC.912.E.7.6); molecular-clock dating (SC.912.L.15.2); reproductive isolation/speciation (.15.9); Hardy–Weinberg conditions/calculations (.15.12); hominid evolution (.15.10–.11); genetic drift/gene flow (.15.14); scientific explanations for the origin of life (.15.8); a comparative account of all four fundamental interactions (SC.912.P.10.10); an explicit comparison of geocentric and heliocentric Solar-System models (SC.8.E.5.8, with heliocentric history present in the 2026 timeline but no located geocentric comparison); electromagnetic-wave field structure and blackbody radiation (SC.912.P.10.17, .10.19); and a detailed recombinant-DNA cloning workflow (SC.912.L.16.12). The NOS review also finds specific 2008 outcomes on pseudoscience and the boundaries of science, general evaluation of information sources, theory/law distinctions, creativity in scientific work, the influence of scientists’ perspectives and productive disagreement, and science–society interaction that are less explicit in the 2026 benchmark packet. Climate, evolution, physics, and genetics remain broadly represented; these are specific methods, mechanisms, examples, or tasks that are less explicit or not located.

NGSS concepts or tasks less explicit in 2026

  • NGSS: the inventory covers all 208 Performance Expectations in the preserved AllTopic document; the human-reviewed comparison table is a selective set of 12 representative PEs, not an exhaustive manual adjudication. Examples of gaps or narrower tasks include evidence for the past-century global temperature rise and the major role of human activity (MS-ESS3-5); use of global climate-model outputs to forecast rates and impacts (HS-ESS3-5); an integrated environmental-change prediction task including new-species emergence (HS-LS4-5); and evidence-based comparison of wave/particle models for electromagnetic radiation (HS-PS4-3). The full crosswalk also records elementary engineering-design tasks, probabilistic weather forecasting, hazard forecastability, and grade-placement changes. Evolution PEs HS-LS4-1–4 are represented across grade 8 and high school, so are classified as retained/relocated rather than omitted. The unreviewed PEs and detailed NGSS appendices remain open comparison work.

Further external standards: gaps and retained comparisons

  • Incremental finding: further external documents add specificity and lineage context, but do not establish another missing broad content area independent of the 2008/NGSS screens. One additional formulation is visible in the Florida Citizens for Science 2007 proposal: it defines evolution explicitly as a change in allele frequencies. Florida 2008 covers Hardy–Weinberg population-genetics calculations and mechanisms, and NGSS HS-LS4-4 refers to change in gene frequency over time; the 2026 draft retains population/trait change but does not state the allele-frequency definition. Record this as an additional framing difference, not as an independent discovery of a missing evolution domain. Reproductive isolation/speciation appears in Florida 2008 as well as California 2006 and Massachusetts 2006; the cloning workflow also appears in Florida 2008 and the 2007 Florida Citizens proposal. Do not count these related-source appearances as separate omission findings.
  • California 2006 and Massachusetts 2006: explicitly teach reproductive/geographic isolation as a mechanism of speciation. This concept also appears in Florida 2008, making it a cross-source specificity gap, not a unique source pathway.
  • Massachusetts 2016: adapts NGSS; it is not an independent source family for shared NGSS content. Its locally stated PE exclusions describe Massachusetts choices, not omissions required in Florida.
  • Florida Citizens for Science 2007: a stakeholder proposal, not an adopted standard. It explicitly frames evolution in allele-frequency terms and includes a detailed cloning workflow. The 2026 draft addresses trait-frequency/population change but does not locate the allele-frequency formulation or technical cloning workflow in the reviewed benchmarks. The proposal states California/Virginia lineage, so these are not independent corroborating pathways.
  • South Carolina 2014: the screened grade-8 climate/evolution concepts are represented or retained in Florida’s draft. Its DNA-replication regulation statement is a content parallel to 2026 SC.912.CB.6.3, but is brief, postdates Florida 2008, and does not establish access or a wording lift.
  • Washington 2009: its grade 6–8 progression on system boundaries, open/closed systems, and matter/energy inputs and outputs is closely paralleled by Florida SC.6.EN.1.1–.3; this is retained content and a sequence comparison, not an omission. The source review found no Washington citation in the preserved Florida 2007/2008 source lists, and no distinctive wording lift.

These are content-scope comparisons, not requirements that Florida adopt another jurisdiction’s curriculum. The further-external-source table in the omission crosswalk labels each case as possible omission, partial, retained, or non-independent; post-2008 sources are not treated as evidence of access during the Florida 2007–08 process.

Earlier Florida standards and GLEs

  • The 1996 high-school standard SC.B.1.4 asks students to consider political and economic factors when selecting energy sources. Those dimensions are less explicit in 2026, although K–5 compares energy-source advantages and high school assesses reliability, cost, and environmental trade-offs.
  • The 1998/99 grades 6–8 GLE SC.D.2.3.2 specifies environmental agencies at grade 7 and legislation protecting Earth systems at grade 8. The 2026 draft retains broad civic participation and policy decision-making in high-school SC.912.ASL.5.2, but does not name the agencies or legislation outcome. This is a partial, upward grade shift in the explicit institutional/legal detail; Florida 2008 also carries related sustainability and environmental-monitoring outcomes. See section 4 of the omission crosswalk.

The separate high-school Earth/space review found 11 NGSS concept links, including mantle convection, Earth-interior models/evidence (HS-ESS2-3), and hazard data/risk/mitigation (MS-ESS3-2). Follow-up review also recorded Florida 2008/1996/GLE concept links for the atmospheric sequence, general scientific inference about interior evidence, geodynamo physics, radiation protection, and compass direction, while keeping the Earth-specific mechanisms visible as added detail. The Earth/space crosswalk records the row-level comparisons and source limits.

Other screened 2008 concepts with clear draft counterparts include mole/stoichiometric calculations, nuclear decay/fission/fusion/half-life, and biodiversity impacts. This bounded screen does not establish that every chemistry, physics, or ecology concept has been reconciled. In the reverse direction, the 2026 Earth geodynamo, magnetosphere, solar-particle protection, and magnetic-navigation sequence extends Florida predecessors. 2008 covers moving charges generating magnetic fields and atmospheric protection; the 1998/99 GLEs mention electromagnets and compass-based direction measurement. These are partial concept matches, not the Earth-specific mechanisms or evidence of source access. NGSS HS-ESS2-3 also uses magnetic-field history as evidence about outer-core convection, but is not a demonstrated textual source.

The nature-of-science comparison treats these as possible scope gaps because the 2026 foreword describes STARS as integrated throughout instruction but supplies no separate coded sequence. The source-first review found some 2008 science/nonscience, pseudoscience, and theory/law distinctions not explicit in the reviewed draft text. This is a scope comparison, not evidence of religious insertion or sectarian intent; the separate full-packet audit found no explicit creationist insertion or denialist rhetoric. Clarifications, examples, and intended teaching materials may carry ideas not stated in benchmark lines, so the candidate list guides follow-up rather than serving as a final declaration of deleted instruction.

Sectarian framing, denialist rhetoric, and scientific uncertainty

The separate full-packet rhetoric audit reviewed all five text extracts, including benchmarks, clarifications, student tasks, examples, foreword, and tables. It found no explicit creationism/intelligent-design insertion and no language saying experts disagree about established findings, scientists have an agenda, methods are inadequate, or scientific institutions should be distrusted. The recognized “bias” hit concerns sampling bias in a methods example; the “God” hit is Robert Goddard.

The draft uses uncertainty and model limitations in conventional ways: students evaluate indirect evidence, compare independent sources, account for variability, revise explanations with new evidence, and recognize peer review and consensus. For example, SC.912.PE.16.2 asks for long-term atmospheric and energy data analysis and assessment of variability/uncertainty, while still naming anthropogenic interactions and evidence. SC.912.SL.1.6 includes peer review and scientific consensus.

The audit separately flags K–5 limits on advanced evolutionary mechanisms and three exclusions of energy-policy analysis/policy advocacy. These may narrow certain curricular discussions and merit a scope review, but the text itself does not establish sectarian motive or denialism.

Semantic retrieval also surfaced a relevant passage in the California 2006 comparison source advising students to “expect contrary opinions” about interpretations of climate data (Grade 6–8 discussion, PDF p.280). That passage is not present in the 2026 draft, and the similarity query did not find a wording match. It is a candidate-source rhetoric lead only; it does not show the Florida writers consulted or adopted it. The source family and query caveat are recorded in semantic-search.md.

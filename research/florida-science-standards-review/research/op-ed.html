Florida’s science draft needs more than a public comment box

These changes also have a history. Florida’s 2008 standards adopted evolution after a contested last-minute wording change, and explicitly asked students to study global climate change. During Rick Scott’s administration, former environmental agency staff told journalists supervisors had warned them against using climate terms in official work; DEP denied having a policy. Under Ron DeSantis, reporting documented state-requested climate edits to textbooks. The episodes are not identical, and none proves what motivated the current workgroup. They do make the current choices about explicit naming and placement important to explain. Florida should do so.

We compared the 2024 Franklin Standards directly with Florida’s October 26 text. Franklin is useful as a scope comparator: it places named expectations in its standards; Florida’s packet places Earth’s and the universe’s approximate ages in supporting guidance, names evolutionary processes in a marine-life benchmark and common ancestry in its clarification, and mentions climate change in a supporting clarification. These are different placements and levels of specificity, not proof Florida copied or adopted Franklin. The text screen found no distinctive extended wording match. A user-supplied LinkedIn link to commentary on Franklin is listed as an inaccessible lead only; its substantive claims do not inform this comparison. The state should publish its source lists and drafting history so the public can test source claims against documents rather than inference. Read the comparison and limits.

Florida’s proposed science standards deserve close public scrutiny. The current draft names evolutionary processes in a marine-life benchmark and includes common ancestry in its clarification; “climate change” appears once in a clarification. The question is what students are explicitly expected to learn, what has been left out, and whether the public can see how the text was produced.

The October 26 second-round draft names evolutionary processes in a high-school benchmark for marine organisms (SC.912.PE.18.1), and its clarification explicitly includes common ancestry. A targeted search did not locate equivalents of the 2008 high-school outcomes on hominid ancestry and fossil evidence of human evolution; the full predecessor crosswalk is pending. The coded text also does not state evolution as a cross-life organizing idea for biology. “Climate change” appears once in a benchmark clarification, not in a coded standard description or benchmark statement; “global warming” is absent from the grade-band packet. A full task-level comparison of climate content against the second-round draft is still underway, so detailed first-round claims about climate mechanisms and human impacts should not be treated as current findings. Some differences are direct in wording. Florida’s 2008 standards asked high-school students to examine human ancestry and fossils showing human evolution. A targeted search of the second-round PDFs did not locate equivalent wording; the full comparison remains pending. The October 26 grade 7 task and clarification give Earth’s age as approximately 4 billion years, and a grade 6 clarification gives the universe’s age as approximately 13.8 billion years. These facts are not in the coded benchmark sentences, but the FDOE foreword says this supporting guidance carries the full weight of expected outcomes. The earlier standards named the Big Bang as the origin of the universe; that phrase is absent from the new grade-band PDFs, which discuss evidence for cosmic expansion. The question is where the state places these connected outcomes and how it expects students to synthesize them.

There is a practical complication for anyone trying to fill the gaps with open curriculum materials. Districts and teachers may give FDOE’s own clarifications and examples more authority than a third-party lesson, even when an outside resource supplies the missing synthesis. That could make a supplement less likely to be adopted or used; we have not measured this. Any effort should map directly to the Florida codes, mark where it adds evidence-based instruction beyond the official wording, and test usability with teachers and district curriculum staff.

That question cannot be answered from PDF order. The draft gives grade levels, but no public calendar showing when each topic should be taught. A genuine review of learning progression also needs a carefully checked map of prerequisite ideas. The current comparison graph tracks which concepts recur across versions; it does not yet identify which ideas must come before others. It would be irresponsible to claim that the draft’s order violates a learning progression until that work is done.

The state’s public process record is also incomplete. The PDFs do not include revision histories or source notes. The 2008 standards published a fuller account of their iterative drafting and review. For the current draft, schedules and comment channels are visible, but dated drafts, meeting notes, comment responses, and change logs have not been located in the reviewed records. Metadata identifies Florida Department of Education staff connected with the exported files, but does not show who authored each benchmark. Nor does a polished document or a Grammarly identifier prove generative AI was used.

These gaps do not prove misconduct. They do prevent the public from checking how major choices were made. Florida should publish the original editable documents, successive drafts, workgroup records, source lists, and responses to public comments. It should also explain how teachers are expected to interpret and prioritize the larger set of coded items and why human evolution no longer appears, why “climate change” appears in clarification rather than in a numbered benchmark, and how the revisions fit the state’s documented history of political disputes over these subjects.

The historical record is grounds for questions, not proof of the 2026 workgroup’s intent. Public review works only when people can see both the proposed text and the path by which it took shape. Florida can make that possible now. Read the source-based historical account and bibliography.

There is also a postsecondary question, but it should be stated precisely. If students are not required to connect evidence to explanations such as evolution or human-caused climate change, preparation may depend more on local courses and teacher choices. Admissions systems can set subject requirements and, in some cases, review high-school courses for eligibility. A 2008 University of California case shows that course-by-course review can exclude a particular class from admission requirements; it does not show that colleges will reject Florida graduates or their science credits. FDOE itself says the new standards should prepare students for college-level science. The draft entered public review only about five weeks ago, so the absence of a located university statement is an early snapshot, not assent or a reason to dismiss the concern. Accreditation is not the likely route: accreditors review colleges and programs, not state K–12 standards. Florida should ask its public universities to publish how draft expectations align with entry-level science preparation and what placement or first-year support would be needed.

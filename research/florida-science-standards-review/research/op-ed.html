Florida’s science draft needs more than a public comment box

These changes also have a history. Florida’s 2008 standards adopted evolution after a contested last-minute wording change, and explicitly asked students to study global climate change. During Rick Scott’s administration, former environmental agency staff told journalists they had been warned against using climate terms. Under Ron DeSantis, reporting documented state-requested climate cuts to textbooks. The episodes are not identical, and none proves what motivated the current workgroup. But they make the present retreat from explicit naming impossible to dismiss as a context-free technical edit. Florida should explain it.

A recent article by Larry Plank argues that Florida’s draft resembles the 2024 Franklin Standards. We compared the NAS model directly with the Florida text. Franklin is useful as a scope comparator: it explicitly names evolution, climate change, Earth’s age, and the universe’s age, where Florida’s draft retains related mechanisms and evidence but leaves some of those expectations less explicit. The text screen found no distinctive extended wording match, and no record establishes that Florida copied or adopted Franklin. The state should publish its source lists and drafting history so the public can test such claims against documents rather than inference. Read the comparison and limits.

Florida’s proposed science standards deserve close public scrutiny. The question is not whether the draft contains any evolution or climate science. It does. The question is what students are explicitly expected to learn, what has been left out, and whether the public can see how the text was produced.

Coded statements retain natural selection, inherited variation, fossil evidence, climate systems, greenhouse processes, and human effects on Earth; supporting clarifications add some discussion of evolutionary relationships. Those facts matter. So do the changes in framing: none of the 1,512 numbered benchmarks uses “evolution” biologically; the sole parent-level use is the geological phrase “landscape evolution.” “Climate change” appears once in a benchmark clarification, not in a coded standard or benchmark statement. “Global warming” is absent from the grade-band packet. Related evidence and mechanisms remain in coded statements, while some relationship language appears only in clarifications; the final rule’s treatment of those materials remains to be verified.

Some omissions are more direct. Florida’s 2008 standards asked high-school students to examine human ancestry and fossils showing human evolution. The new draft has no equivalent expectation. It retains Earth science and teaches radiometric dating, but asks students to date rocks and events without explicitly asking how scientists determine Earth’s age. The earlier standards named the Big Bang as the origin of the universe; the new standards discuss evidence for an expanding universe but leave out that name and give no age for the universe. Each difference deserves an answer from the state, especially when students’ scientific understanding depends on connecting evidence to the explanations it supports.

Leaf-benchmark wording alone is about 16,403 words in the proposal and 15,675 in 2008 (+4.6%). Including coded parent descriptions on both sides, the totals are about 23,712 and 18,875 words (+25.6%). The registers contain 1,512 and 498 items, but three times as many codes does not automatically mean three times as many distinct teachable targets; the frameworks may bundle and divide expectations differently. The 2026 PDFs add substantial benchmark-linked guidance—clarifications, examples, and student tasks—so the 216,632-word grade-band and 239,671-word five-PDF totals describe the review packet, not the coded standards-and-benchmarks text. The larger coded-text count is about 25.6% above 2008 under the method described in the source-layer analysis. This material can still affect interpretation and teacher preparation. It is not a full curriculum, and its length alone cannot establish classroom workload.

There is a practical complication for anyone trying to fill the gaps with open curriculum materials. Districts and teachers may give FDOE’s own clarifications and examples more authority than a third-party lesson, even when an outside resource supplies the missing synthesis. That could make a supplement less likely to be adopted or used; we have not measured this. Any effort should map directly to the Florida codes, mark where it adds evidence-based instruction beyond the official wording, and test usability with teachers and district curriculum staff.

That question cannot be answered from PDF order. The draft gives grade levels, but no public calendar showing when each topic should be taught. A genuine review of learning progression also needs a carefully checked map of prerequisite ideas. The current comparison graph tracks which concepts recur across versions; it does not yet identify which ideas must come before others. It would be irresponsible to claim that the draft’s order violates a learning progression until that work is done.

The state’s public process record is also incomplete. The PDFs do not include revision histories or source notes. The 2008 standards published a fuller account of their iterative drafting and review. For the current draft, schedules and comment channels are visible, but dated drafts, meeting notes, comment responses, and change logs have not been located in the reviewed records. Metadata identifies Florida Department of Education staff connected with the exported files, but does not show who authored each benchmark. Nor does a polished document or a Grammarly identifier prove generative AI was used.

These gaps do not prove misconduct. They do prevent the public from checking how major choices were made. Florida should publish the original editable documents, successive drafts, workgroup records, source lists, and responses to public comments. It should also explain how teachers are expected to interpret and prioritize the larger set of coded items and why human evolution no longer appears, why “climate change” is absent from the numbered benchmarks, and how the revisions fit the state’s documented history of political disputes over these subjects.

The historical record is grounds for questions, not proof of the 2026 workgroup’s intent. Public review works only when people can see both the proposed text and the path by which it took shape. Florida can make that possible now. Read the source-based historical account and bibliography.

There is also a postsecondary question, but it should be stated precisely. If students are not required to connect evidence to explanations such as evolution or human-caused climate change, preparation may depend more on local courses and teacher choices. Admissions systems can set subject requirements and, in some cases, review high-school courses for eligibility. A 2008 University of California case shows that course-by-course review can exclude a particular class from admission requirements; it does not show that colleges will reject Florida graduates or their science credits. Accreditation is not the likely route: accreditors review colleges and programs, not state K–12 standards. Florida should ask its public universities to publish how draft expectations align with entry-level science preparation and what placement or first-year support would be needed.

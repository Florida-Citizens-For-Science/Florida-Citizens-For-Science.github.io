Florida’s proposed science standards: what is changing, what is missing, and what remains unknown

Plain-language brief for journalists and general readers · October 29, 2026

Why the wording and placement matter

This is not an isolated vocabulary change. In 2008, Florida adopted evolution after a contested last-minute wording change; the same standards explicitly named global climate change. Later reporting described climate-language restrictions in state agencies under Rick Scott and state-requested climate edits to textbooks under Ron DeSantis. Florida has released a large draft of new K–12 science standards for public review. The draft names evolutionary processes in a marine-life benchmark and includes common ancestry in its clarification; a task-level comparison is complete and identifies substantial evidence-analysis of human influences, alongside narrower task-framing differences from NGSS. But it changes how some subjects are named and organized, leaves out several specific expectations found in the 2008 standards, and arrives without the draft history needed to trace who proposed individual passages or how they changed.

This review compares the publicly available draft with Florida’s 2008 standards, surviving 1996 standards and grade expectations, and selected national and state frameworks. It does not claim to know what happened in private meetings or whether any person or outside organization wrote particular passages. The complete comparison, source list, and data tables are available in the full report.

What the draft teaches about evolution

The draft does not eliminate natural selection. Grade 8 standards ask students to describe what natural selection requires, explain how inherited variation affects survival and reproduction, and investigate changes in traits across populations. Students also analyze fossils and other evidence that life has changed over time. High-school coded statements cover population variation/change, mutations, and adaptation. Supporting clarifications discuss evolutionary and phylogenetic relationships; those appear outside the coded standard descriptions and benchmark statements.

There is, however, a significant change in framing and scope. In the October 26 second-round packet, a high-school benchmark names “evolutionary processes” in relation to marine organisms (SC.912.PE.18.1), and its clarification includes common ancestry. The coded benchmark is limited to diversity, adaptation, and distribution of marine organisms. A targeted search did not locate equivalents of the 2008 high-school expectations about hominid ancestry and what fossil hominids show about human evolution; the exact cross-version ID/text diff and bounded predecessor review are complete; the latter found continuity or partial matches in most rows, but did not identify those specific human-evolution tasks. The 2008 standards called evolution the organizing principle of life science. The current draft does not state that broader organizing role in a coded benchmark.

The 2008 standards explicitly asked high-school students to identify trends in hominid ancestry and to discuss what fossil hominids show about human evolution. The bounded review also did not locate those tasks among its selected predecessor candidates, but it is not an exhaustive search of all possible source materials. This comparison cannot establish why the writers made it.

The concern is about how those ideas are organized: the marine-life benchmark names evolutionary processes and its clarification includes common ancestry, while a targeted search did not locate the 2008 human-ancestry and fossil-hominid outcomes in the current PDFs. The National Academies’ K–12 framework treats common ancestry and multiple lines of evidence as part of biological evolution across life. The exact ID/text comparison and bounded review of 85 revised or new/no-exact sentences are complete: 40 continuity, 38 partial matches, 7 candidate-specificity gaps, and 10 unresolved; 19 reindexes were assessed separately. No equivalent human-evolution task was located in this scoped review. The instructional implications are a risk to examine, not a measured classroom outcome. Read the framework’s evolution chapter.

Climate wording and placement

The October 26 climate comparison is complete at the task level. Grade 8 and high school ask students to analyze evidence about climate-pattern changes, human influence, atmospheric and energy processes, long-term data, and uncertainty. The narrower differences are that the draft does not state NGSS’s past-century temperature-rise/major-human-role task or global-model-based forecasting of rates and impacts in the same explicit form. See the source-checked climate task register; these differences do not establish climate-content absence.

At the same time, the terminology changes. “Climate change” appears once in the October 26 grade-band documents, in a benchmark clarification that lists it as one human impact on mammals. It does not appear in a coded standard description or numbered benchmark statement. “Global warming” does not appear in the second-round grade-band packet. By contrast, the 2008 benchmarks used “climate change” in four statements; one explicitly asked how Earth-system and astronomical factors contribute to global climate change.

The current placement finding is narrower than the first-round comparison: “climate change” appears once in an October 26 benchmark clarification, not in a coded standard description or numbered benchmark. The 2008 standards used the phrase in four benchmark statements, including one asking students to relate Earth-system and astronomical factors to global climate change. The October 26 task-level comparison is complete: human-influence evidence analysis is present, while NGSS’s past-century attribution and model-based future-projection tasks are not stated in the same explicit form. The seven-row audit provides locators and scope limits. See the separate Franklin comparison.

Age information and universe origin across layers

The October 26 second-round packet says Earth is approximately 4 billion years old in a grade 7 task example and clarification (SC.7.PE.3.2, PDF p. 85). The coded benchmark sentence itself is broader: “Recognize that Earth’s history is organized using the geologic time scale.” So the Earth-age claim is present in the current proposal’s supporting guidance; it is not accurate to describe Earth’s age as wholly omitted. The 6–8 foreword says such clarifications and examples carry the full weight of expected outcomes.

The packet also says the universe is approximately 13.8 billion years old in a grade 6 clarification (SC.6.SP.1.2, PDF p. 99). The Big Bang phrase does not appear in the three October 26 grade-band PDFs. The 2008 high-school standard named the Big Bang as the universe’s origin and asked for supporting evidence. This is a difference in naming and placement: the current supporting guidance gives the universe’s age, while the coded text and the packet overall do not name the Big Bang. The standards remain proposed; their final legal status depends on the rule and incorporated files. Read the updated source-first comparison.

Benchmark statements and supporting guidance

Size comparison update: the reproducible standards-body count is 222,031 word tokens for the October 26 Florida packet and 17,237 for the 2008 standards body, a 12.9-fold text-volume difference. Current benchmark-linked guidance, examples, and tasks are included because FDOE says they carry full weight; the 2008 body has no parallel layer. This measures text volume, not distinct target count, class hours, or workload. See the size method and source comparisons.

The October 26 packet includes clarifications, examples, and sample tasks around the benchmarks. It can shape teachers’ interpretation and preparation; its eventual legal status depends on the final rule and incorporated science document. It does not provide a full lesson sequence, calendar, or complete set of instructional materials. A workload judgment requires classroom-time and teacher-planning evidence.

This distinction matters for filling the identified gaps. An independent supplement may be less persuasive or harder to adopt when FDOE’s own examples and clarifications are seen as the authoritative interpretation. That is a risk to investigate with teachers and district curriculum staff, not a finding about their current practice. A useful supplement would map each lesson to Florida’s benchmark codes, label added synthesis clearly, and show the evidence supporting it.

College preparation and admissions: a risk to investigate

FDOE says the new standards should prepare students for college-level science. That gives the state and universities a concrete alignment question: do the required outcomes prepare students to connect evidence to major explanations? Students whose schools do not add missing synthesis may receive less consistent preparation for later science study. Some admissions systems review high-school courses against their own criteria. In a 2008 case, the University of California excluded certain courses from its freshman-admission subject requirements, including a biology course that dismissed evolution. That was one system’s review of particular private-school courses—not a Florida standards dispute, a refusal of college-earned credit, or an accreditor action. We have not located such a statement in the roughly five weeks since the draft entered public review. That short window makes silence inconclusive; it is not evidence of assent or lack of concern. Accreditation generally covers colleges and programs, not state K–12 standards. The supported concern is possible uneven preparation, not a prediction of denied admission or credit. Read the evidence and limits.

Does the order make educational sense?

The draft assigns expectations to grades, and its PDF lists them in an order. Neither feature proves that teachers are expected to teach them in that order. A pacing guide or calendar is needed to judge which concepts students encounter first during the school year.

The current research graph connects standards to shared concepts and possible predecessors. It was built to study content continuity and provenance; it does not yet encode a reviewed map of which ideas students must understand before others. That prerequisite map is necessary to test whether presentation order runs against a defensible learning progression. Didactopus, a curriculum knowledge-graph project, has tools for checking prerequisite graphs, but its structural checks cannot supply the missing educational judgments or infer the intended teaching calendar. For now, a claim that the draft violates a coherent learning progression would go beyond the evidence.

What we know about the drafting process

The draft is published as five PDFs. Their metadata names Florida Department of Education staff members associated with the exported files, but those fields do not identify who wrote individual standards. The files contain no tracked revisions, comments, or source bibliography. Two contain a Grammarly document identifier; that shows a document-processing trace, not use of generative writing or evidence of outside access.

Florida’s 2008 standards included a detailed account of the writing and review process. The records located for the current process show a planned weekly meeting schedule, a workgroup call, and public comment arrangements, but not a set of dated drafts, meeting minutes, comment dispositions, or change logs. Without those records, the public cannot reconstruct how the new text developed.

A comparison found three distinctive wording lineages: two draft passages match wording in the NGSS/National Research Council family, and one short phrase matches the earlier Florida standards. These matches show that the wording has a history; they do not identify the person who brought it in or prove improper access. The broad concept comparisons do not establish copying.

What reporters and families can ask

The most useful next records are the original Word files with comments and tracked changes; dated versions and version-management logs; meeting agendas and minutes; written comments and responses; workgroup rosters and submitted materials; source lists for each benchmark; and official pacing guidance. Those materials could distinguish ordinary editing from substantive changes, identify how concerns were handled, and show whether external references or AI tools played a role.

The findings here are specific, but they are not a verdict on motive. We distinguish coded benchmark wording from clarifications, examples, and tasks: the current packet names common ancestry and both ages in supporting guidance, A targeted search did not locate equivalents of the 2008 human-evolution outcomes, but the exact ID/text and bounded predecessor reviews are complete, with 11 residual rows unresolved; the Big Bang name is absent from the current PDFs. FDOE says supporting guidance carries the full weight of expected outcomes; the adopted rule and incorporated files will determine final legal status. Earlier first-round coded-text totals and percentages remain withdrawn; the new matched standards-body size comparison is reproducible and documented; see the source-layer analysis. Those public-policy choices merit examination on their own terms. The missing process record leaves important questions open. Florida should publish the underlying drafts and let reviewers compare the evidence.

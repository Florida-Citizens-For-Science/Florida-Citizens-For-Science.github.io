Florida’s proposed science standards: what is changing, what is missing, and what remains unknown

Plain-language brief for journalists and general readers · October 29, 2026

Why these omissions are headline findings

This is not an isolated vocabulary change. In 2008, Florida adopted evolution after a contested last-minute wording change; the same standards explicitly named global climate change. Later news investigations reported climate-term restrictions in state agencies under Rick Scott and climate-content edits requested for textbooks under Ron DeSantis. No coded 2026 statement uses “evolution” biologically; the lone parent-level use is “landscape evolution” in a geological context. “Climate change” occurs once in a benchmark clarification, not in coded standard or benchmark text. Natural selection, fossils, climate mechanisms, and human Earth-system effects remain, so the finding is about diminished naming and required synthesis, not total removal. The historical record warrants scrutiny; it does not establish the 2026 writers’ motive. See the evidence note and project bibliography.

Florida has released a large draft of new K–12 science standards for public review. The draft still teaches many of the core ideas people are asking about: natural selection, fossils, climate systems, greenhouse processes, and human effects on Earth. But it changes how some subjects are named and organized, leaves out several specific expectations found in the 2008 standards, and arrives without the draft history needed to trace who proposed individual passages or how they changed.

This review compares the publicly available draft with Florida’s 2008 standards, surviving 1996 standards and grade expectations, and selected national and state frameworks. It does not claim to know what happened in private meetings or whether any person or outside organization wrote particular passages. The complete comparison, source list, and data tables are available in the full report.

Franklin Standards: tested, not assumed

A LinkedIn article argues that Florida’s draft resembles the 2024 Franklin Standards. We compared the NAS model with the Florida text: Franklin explicitly names evolution, climate change, Earth’s age, and the universe’s age, while Florida retains related mechanisms and evidence but states some named expectations less explicitly. The text screen found no distinctive extended match, and the records do not establish copying or adoption. Read the source-based comparison · See both references.

What the draft teaches about evolution

The draft does not eliminate natural selection. Grade 8 standards ask students to describe what natural selection requires, explain how inherited variation affects survival and reproduction, and investigate changes in traits across populations. Students also analyze fossils and other evidence that life has changed over time. High-school coded statements cover population variation/change, mutations, and adaptation. Supporting clarifications discuss evolutionary and phylogenetic relationships; those appear outside the coded standard descriptions and benchmark statements.

There is, however, a significant change in framing. None of the 1,512 numbered benchmark statements in the three grade-band PDFs uses the word “evolution.” A parent-level statement uses “evolution” in the geological phrase “landscape evolution” (SC.912.EE.4); other appearances occur in supporting text. The coded statements do not name biological evolution, common ancestry, or human evolution. The 2008 standards used “evolution” in ten benchmark statements and described it as the organizing principle of life science. The 2026 draft also does not name common descent—shared ancestry—as the explanation that unifies evidence about how living things are related. That omission matters because students can be asked to recognize evidence and patterns without being asked to connect them to the central explanation that evolutionary biology draws from them. The Franklin comparison shows that another contemporary model names evolution as a grade-level expectation; it does not show that Florida used or rejected that model.

The draft also drops two explicit 2008 expectations about human evolution. Those standards asked high-school students to identify trends in hominid ancestry and to discuss what fossil hominids show about human evolution. No equivalent expectation was located in the 2026 draft. This is a concrete content omission, not merely a change in terminology. It is consistent with a longstanding sectarian objection to human common ancestry, though the omission alone cannot show why the writers made it.

The concern is cumulative: students may meet natural selection, fossils, and genetic relationships without the benchmarks asking them to synthesize these lines of evidence as a shared history of life, including humans. The National Academies’ K–12 framework treats common ancestry and multiple lines of evidence as part of the core idea of biological evolution. Teachers can supply the missing connections, but leaving that work to local choices can make a coherent, scientifically complete account less consistent across classrooms. This is a curricular risk, not a claim that every Florida teacher will teach the topic inaccurately. Read the framework’s evolution chapter.

Climate science is present, but the words are less visible

Climate instruction is not absent. Middle-school students distinguish weather from climate and study factors that shape climate patterns. High-school standards address Earth’s energy balance, greenhouse processes, atmospheric and ocean circulation, climate feedback, and the effects of human activity on Earth systems. Students are asked to evaluate evidence and models.

At the same time, the terminology changes. “Climate change” appears once in the three grade-band documents, in a benchmark clarification that lists it as one human impact on mammals. It does not appear in a coded standard description or numbered benchmark statement. “Global warming” does not appear in the 2026 grade-band packet. By contrast, the 2008 benchmarks used “climate change” in three statements and explicitly asked how Earth-system and astronomical factors contribute to global climate change.

This word-count finding does not mean climate science has vanished: the draft includes extensive climate content under other headings. It does mean the key public terms have been moved out of the central statements or removed. That may affect what is emphasized, searched, taught, or assessed. The draft also does not state the 2008 task that links external astronomical forcing to global climate change, or the national standards task that connects long-term temperature records to human influence. These are specific differences for reviewers to examine, not proof that climate evidence is generally rejected. Franklin offers a separate, explicit climate-change standard; Florida’s draft includes mechanisms and human-system effects but does not clearly require the same integrated attribution task. See the comparison.

For students, the missing piece is the required synthesis: connect long-term observations with evidence about natural and human influences, then use models to reason about future change. The draft does ask students to evaluate evidence about human interactions with atmospheric and energy systems, but does not clearly require the century-scale attribution and model-based forecasting tasks found in national standards. If curriculum and assessments do not fill the gap, students may be left to assemble the full connection on their own, and access may vary by classroom. The 2024 U.S. Climate Literacy Guide likewise connects climate-system observations, modeling, natural drivers, and human-caused recent change. This comparison identifies a risk to coherence; it does not establish that the draft rejects climate science or that teachers cannot provide the connection.

Earth’s age and the history of the universe

The 2008 grade 7 standards explicitly ask students to identify methods used to measure the age of Earth. The 2026 draft teaches relative dating and radiometric dating, but its high-school benchmark asks students to use those methods to determine the numerical ages of rocks and events, not to establish Earth’s age as a whole. This is a shift in application and grade placement, not the removal of dating science.

The 2008 high-school standards name the Big Bang as the origin of the universe and ask students to cite evidence for the theory. The 2026 draft instead asks students to analyze evidence for an expanding universe, including galaxy redshift and cosmic background radiation. The important evidence remains, but the Big Bang label and an explicit statement of the universe’s age are absent. The 2008 benchmark did not specify a numerical age either, so the comparison is about what the standards require students to explain—not about deleting a number Florida previously required them to memorize. The 2024 Franklin Standards do state approximate ages for Earth and the universe; Florida’s draft does not. That comparison adds a concrete age-of-Earth and universe-age benchmark without changing what the 2008 text required. Read the source note.

Similar benchmark wording, more supporting guidance

The leaf-benchmark comparison is about 16,403 words in 2026 and 15,675 in 2008 (+4.6%). Including parent standard descriptions on both sides gives about 23,712 versus 18,875 words (+25.6%); this is the broader comparison of coded standards-and-benchmarks text. The registers contain 1,512 versus 498 numbered items, but different coding granularity means that is not a direct count of teachable targets. The three 2026 grade-band PDFs total 216,632 extracted words because they include benchmark-linked clarifications, examples, student tasks, and instructional notes as well as the benchmark text. The separate foreword and tables add 23,039 words. The 239,671-word total describes the five-PDF review packet, not the coded standards-and-benchmarks text or a complete curriculum. The word-count method and adoption distinction are documented in the source-layer analysis.

The added material includes clarifications, examples, and sample tasks around the benchmarks. It can shape teachers’ interpretation and preparation; its eventual legal status depends on the final rule and incorporated science document. It does not provide a full lesson sequence, calendar, or complete set of instructional materials. Page totals are affected by layout and repeated text, and none of these counts establishes that teaching the draft takes a particular number of hours. A workload judgment requires classroom-time and teacher-planning evidence.

This distinction matters for filling the identified gaps. An independent supplement may be less persuasive or harder to adopt when FDOE’s own examples and clarifications are seen as the authoritative interpretation. That is a risk to investigate with teachers and district curriculum staff, not a finding about their current practice. A useful supplement would map each lesson to Florida’s benchmark codes, label added synthesis clearly, and show the evidence supporting it.

College preparation and admissions: a risk to investigate

FDOE says the new standards should prepare students for college-level science. That gives the state and universities a concrete alignment question: do the required outcomes prepare students to connect evidence to major explanations? Students whose schools do not add missing synthesis may receive less consistent preparation for later science study. Some admissions systems review high-school courses against their own criteria. In a 2008 case, the University of California excluded certain courses from its freshman-admission subject requirements, including a biology course that dismissed evolution. That was one system’s review of particular private-school courses—not a Florida standards dispute, a refusal of college-earned credit, or an accreditor action. We have not located such a statement in the roughly five weeks since the draft entered public review. That short window makes silence inconclusive; it is not evidence of assent or lack of concern. Accreditation generally covers colleges and programs, not state K–12 standards. The supported concern is possible uneven preparation, not a prediction of denied admission or credit. Read the evidence and limits.

Does the order make educational sense?

The draft assigns expectations to grades, and its PDF lists them in an order. Neither feature proves that teachers are expected to teach them in that order. A pacing guide or calendar is needed to judge which concepts students encounter first during the school year.

The current research graph connects standards to shared concepts and possible predecessors. It was built to study content continuity and provenance; it does not yet encode a reviewed map of which ideas students must understand before others. That prerequisite map is necessary to test whether presentation order runs against a defensible learning progression. Didactopus, a curriculum knowledge-graph project, has tools for checking prerequisite graphs, but its structural checks cannot supply the missing educational judgments or infer the intended teaching calendar. For now, a claim that the draft violates a coherent learning progression would go beyond the evidence.

What we know about the drafting process

The draft is published as five PDFs. Their metadata names Florida Department of Education staff members associated with the exported files, but those fields do not identify who wrote individual standards. The files contain no tracked revisions, comments, or source bibliography. Two contain a Grammarly document identifier; that shows a document-processing trace, not use of generative writing or evidence of outside access.

Florida’s 2008 standards included a detailed account of the writing and review process. The records located for the current process show a planned weekly meeting schedule, a workgroup call, and public comment arrangements, but not a set of dated drafts, meeting minutes, comment dispositions, or change logs. Without those records, the public cannot reconstruct how the new text developed.

A comparison found three distinctive wording lineages: two draft passages match wording in the NGSS/National Research Council family, and one short phrase matches the earlier Florida standards. These matches show that the wording has a history; they do not identify the person who brought it in or prove improper access. The broad concept comparisons do not establish copying.

What reporters and families can ask

The most useful next records are the original Word files with comments and tracked changes; dated versions and version-management logs; meeting agendas and minutes; written comments and responses; workgroup rosters and submitted materials; source lists for each benchmark; and official pacing guidance. Those materials could distinguish ordinary editing from substantive changes, identify how concerns were handled, and show whether external references or AI tools played a role.

The findings here are specific, but they are not a verdict on motive. The primary omission claims use coded standard descriptions and numbered benchmarks; the one “climate change” clarification is reported separately. The final rule and its incorporated files must be checked to determine the adopted status of embedded guidance. Count data: JSON. See the source-layer analysis. The draft retains important science instruction, while changing terminology, omitting human-evolution expectations, narrowing some applications, and expanding the amount of supporting text. Those are public-policy choices worth examining on their own terms. The missing process record leaves important questions open. The best way to answer them is to publish the underlying drafts and let reviewers compare the evidence.

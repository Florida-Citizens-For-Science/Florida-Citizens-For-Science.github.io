Public notice and reactions to the 2026 draft

FDOE’s listening tour and first public-review round began in September 2026. A separate formal rulemaking comment period opened November 23, with a December 14 deadline; the current FDOE portal continues to list second-round drafts dated October 26. Notices, session recordings, local reporting, and organizational commentary document a debate over explicit science expectations, instructional load, scientific inquiry, and public participation.

What the notice establishes: FDOE’s public portal labels the review period “September – October 2026,” and currently lists four listening opportunities, the last held October 27. It does not state a specific day or time for written comments to close. An October 16 NCSE page reports that comments were being accepted through the end of October; this is a secondary report, not a deadline statement on the FDOE portal. Reporting published October 29 says the public still had an opportunity to shape the draft before expected State Board consideration in December. Check the current FDOE portal for any update.

November evidence check: revision advice and reactions

On November 15, physicist Paul Cottle described his participation in the middle-school revision subgroup. He says the group recommended adding “biological evolution” in five locations and that the October 26 draft did not incorporate that phrase in its grades 6–8 standards. His reproduced November 8 message identifies two standards, three benchmarks, and proposed clarifications. This is a participant’s firsthand account of his own experience and request; the subgroup’s underlying submission and full membership have not been independently obtained. His assessment that mechanisms already appear is distinct from a request to name them explicitly.

Education Week, November 19, reports retired teacher and 2008 standards contributor David Campbell’s concern and quotes NCSE’s Glenn Branch on the fragmented treatment of evolution and climate. The NCSE statement of November 20 discusses the same report and argues that the revision remains inadequate. These are attributed assessments, not a representative survey. Education Week says FDOE did not answer its questions about Mack’s statement or the drafting process.

FDOE’s meeting schedule lists a December 16 meeting at 9 a.m. Central in Panama City, subject to change; the schedule itself does not identify a science agenda item. Cottle and NCSE anticipate science consideration then, but their predictions and Cottle’s anticipated handoff are not verification of an agenda or an adoption. The October 1 rule-development notice concerns mathematics and science revisions; it is not itself a final rule. Checked November 21; searches and inaccessible records may miss communications. How notice and coverage unfolded

  1. September 25 — FDOE launches the review. The Department announced a “comprehensive rewrite,” described its goals as coherent, content-rich, evidence-based science standards, and announced three listening sessions. WUSF reported that the first proposed PDFs were posted that Friday. The current portal lists second-round drafts dated October 26 and an October 27 Orange County opportunity. These are review drafts, not approved standards.
  2. September 28–October 1 — the first reporting and meetings. WUSF previewed the input opportunities and reported concern among education advocates that climate-change instruction could be reduced. WLRN’s October 1 coverage of the meetings reported disagreement: some participants said underlying concepts remained despite terminology changes; others objected to the absence of familiar terms and asked for explicit evolution and climate expectations. It also included an educator who welcomed emphasis on modeling, data, laboratory practice, and evidence.
  3. October 9–15 — comment guidance and a public defense. Educator Bertha Vazquez published a step-by-step guide encouraging comments about evolution and climate language. At an October 14 State Board meeting, Commissioner Henry Mack defended the draft’s emphasis on evidence and non-ideological science and said evolution and climate science remained in the standards. Florida Phoenix reporting, republished by WLRN, also quoted the Florida Education Association’s call for evidence-led standards, broader participation, and more time for review.
  4. October 27–29 — final listening session and updated responses. The Florida Department of State published an October 14 meeting notice for the October 27 Orange County session. An October 28 NCSE account said reporting had not identified the revisions FDOE said it had made after receiving nearly 1,800 comments. Central Florida Public Media’s October 29 report described Commissioner Mack’s defense and summarized criticism about explicit evolution and human-caused climate-change expectations, nature-of-science language, and workload. It also reported support from attendees who favored questioning models and respectful disagreement.

Reading the record carefully

  • FDOE’s portal identifies the draft as proposed and not approved; the official October 27 meeting notice identifies the subject as “Science State Academic Standards.”
  • As of this October 31 check, the official portal gave a month-range rather than an exact written-comment closing time. NCSE reported on October 16 that comments were accepted through the end of October, and WUSF reported that public input was still being accepted at the October 27 session. This was the listening-tour review window. A separate formal rulemaking period opened November 23, 2026 and closes December 14; see Notice 31634045 above.
  • The reported “nearly 1,800” figure is the Department’s comment count as relayed at the listening session. The sources reviewed here do not provide the submissions, a breakdown by subject, or a public response matrix showing which revisions followed which comments.
  • News stories select representative speakers; organization pages express the organization’s position. Neither supplies a representative poll or a full transcript of every comment. The Florida Channel’s September 30 recording is a primary audiovisual record of the virtual public hearing.
  • The reactions inform public context. They do not replace the benchmark-by-benchmark comparison or establish the motives behind the drafting decisions.

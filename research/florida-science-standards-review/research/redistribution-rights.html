Public access is clear; redistribution rights are not settled

Scope: this review concerns Florida’s November 20, 2026 proposed science standards (FLRules Ref-20438), not every file on FDOE’s standards portal. It summarizes statutes and published decisions; it is not a legal opinion about a specific enforcement dispute.

Finding: Florida law does not support treating all government-provided documents as freely redistributable merely because they are public records. Florida appellate precedent generally bars a government office from claiming copyright in its own public records without specific statutory authority. But Florida Statutes §1006.39 specifically addresses educational materials developed by or under the Department of Education and authorizes the Department to publish, sell, license, and protect covered materials. Whether that authority applies to this proposed standards document, and how it interacts with the rule text’s status as an official government edict, has not been resolved for Ref-20438 in the sources reviewed. The DOCX contains no express copyright notice, but that alone does not settle the issue.

What Florida’s open-government laws do

“Sunshine Law” can refer to two different parts of Florida’s open-government framework. The Government-in-the-Sunshine Law, Florida Statutes §286.011, concerns open meetings. Public access to agency records is principally governed by Article I, §24 of the Florida Constitution and Chapter 119. Section 119.01 states that state and local records are open for inspection and copying; §119.07 sets out the custodian’s duties, copying fees, and exemptions. Those provisions establish access and copying rights. By themselves, they do not answer every later copyright question about redistribution.

How the rule developed

  • 1892 and 1905: In State ex rel. Davis v. McMillan, the Florida Supreme Court applied the 1892 records statutes to allow abstract-company employees to inspect records and make extracts for a business that sold abstracts. The court rejected additional charges beyond fees authorized by law.
  • 1967 and 1992: Florida enacted its open-meetings law in 1967. Voters added the constitutional right to inspect or copy public records and attend public meetings in 1992; the records provision took effect July 1, 1993. The constitutional text permits only narrowly tailored statutory exemptions enacted by a two-thirds vote of each legislative house.
  • 2004–05: In Microdecisions, Inc. v. Skinner, the Second District Court of Appeal held that a county property appraiser could not copyright office-produced GIS maps or use a license to restrict commercial redistribution without statutory authorization. The Florida Supreme Court denied review in 2005. The 2024 Attorney General’s Sunshine Manual repeats the rule, while also distinguishing government-produced works from copyrighted materials received from outside owners.

Why the Department of Education is a special case

Florida Statutes §1006.39 covers educational materials and products developed by or under FDOE’s direction. It says the Department may publish or produce them, may sell copies to the public, may license or assign use in specified circumstances, and must protect covered materials against unlawful use or infringement. That is a specific statutory authority relevant to FDOE materials. A separate provision, §119.084, specifically authorizes copyright of agency-created data-processing software.

FDOE has used §1006.39 to state reuse limits on at least some educational publications: a 2003 FDOE publication’s copyright page allows reproduction within Florida’s public education system and says reproduction or distribution outside it requires prior written approval. That example shows the statute has practical use, but it does not establish that FDOE claimed copyright in Ref-20438. The November 20 DOCX has no comparable notice. Nor does the notice’s inclusion in FLRules itself grant an open license.

Application to Ref-20438

QuestionWhat the reviewed record supports
Is the document a public record?Yes. It is an official proposed-rule document published by FLRules and connected to FDOE rulemaking.
Can the public inspect and obtain a copy?Yes, subject to ordinary statutory limits and fees; FLRules provides the source file directly.
Can FDOE restrict redistribution?Not on the basis of a copyright claim alone if no law authorizes that claim. But §1006.39 is a plausible specific authorization for Department-developed educational materials. No reviewed court decision resolves whether it covers this proposed standards text, and the source file has no express reuse statement.
Does adopted rule text have a different status?Potentially. The U.S. Supreme Court’s government-edicts doctrine bars copyright in certain works produced by officials empowered to speak with the force of law. Whether the final rule text fits that doctrine, and how that interacts with §1006.39, has not been decided for these standards. Ref-20438 is still a proposal.

Accordingly, this review does not conclude that the government can prohibit all redistribution, or that this document is unrestricted. It also does not find an explicit FDOE copyright claim on Ref-20438. The specific §1006.39 authorization and unresolved scope are enough that the site should not present the full DOCX or a full-text extraction as cleared for unrestricted redistribution. The current source link, local checksum, change counts, coded locators, and analytical notes remain available. Public change registers do not reproduce complete before-and-after standards passages.

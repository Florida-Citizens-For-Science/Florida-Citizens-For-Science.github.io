2026 Florida science draft: sectarian and denialist-rhetoric audit

The 2008 wording fight matters to this audit

Florida adopted evolution in 2008 after a 4–3 vote and a contested last-minute qualifier; the same standards described evolution as biology’s organizing principle. Later climate-language restrictions were reported under Rick Scott, and textbook climate edits were reported under Ron DeSantis. This history makes the current missing synthesis politically consequential, but it is contextual evidence, not proof of the 2026 drafters’ motive. See the verified chronology and bibliography.

Audit date: October 22, 2026
Scope: All five locally preserved 2026 packet text extracts: K–5, grades 6–8, grades 9–12, K–12 tables, and foreword. Benchmarks, clarifications, examples, student tasks, and supplemental tables were included. This is a bounded textual audit of the public draft, not an inference about individual authors, motive, classroom implementation, or unprovided versions.

Findings

Sectarian insertion

The first-round text search found no overtly religious phrase or explicit creationist doctrine in that version. Its searches for creation/creationism, intelligent design, teach the controversy, academic freedom, faith, religion, Bible/biblical, God, creator, common ancestry, and common descent returned no relevant first-round content. The October 26 version does include “common ancestry” in the SC.912.PE.18.1 clarification; other findings here remain first-round unless explicitly rechecked. That bounded keyword result does not settle whether wording and placement are consequential.

This is a negative finding about explicit wording. It cannot establish that an unstated religious influence did not affect drafting, and the omission of a science/religion discussion found in the 2008 text does not itself show sectarian intent.

Process-context evidence outside the standards: FDOE’s September 25, 2026 press release launching the listening tour quotes Commissioner Henry Mack invoking the classical tradition and saying “the book of creation can be read by reason”; it also calls for classrooms to focus on the dignity of life and cultivation of virtue. The full press release is preserved in the process corpus. This is relevant evidence of the administration’s public framing, including language with religious resonance. It is not wording found in the reviewed draft standards and does not show that religious content was inserted or that the workgroup adopted this framing. The distinction prevents a negative standards-text scan from being mistaken for a finding that the surrounding process had no religiously inflected rhetoric.

Rhetoric about scientific disagreement, uncertainty, methods, and trust

No reviewed passage says that experts or scientists disagree about established findings; alleges that scientists have an agenda or bias; directs readers to distrust scientists, scientific institutions, or peer review; calls a scientific conclusion inconclusive or unsupported; or frames scientific uncertainty as proof that a field is unreliable. Searches for disagreement, controversy, agenda, distrust, fraud, fabrication, manipulation, ideology, conflicts of interest, and false-balance language returned no substantive hits. The substring bias appears in a laboratory-methods example about reducing sampling bias, not bias among scientists.

The draft does contain recurring, conventional science-literacy instruction about measurement error, uncertainty, model limitations, indirect evidence, interpretation, and revision. In context these passages ask students to evaluate evidence, compare independent sources, refine models, and distinguish observations from inferences. They do not use uncertainty to discredit a field.

Evolution and treatment of religion compared with the 2008 standards

The draft has several K–5 scope exclusions that merit accurate description but do not, standing alone, show sectarian shading:

  • SC.1.PE.1.6, clarification 4: “Instruction excludes advanced classification or evolutionary processes” (fl-k5.txt, around line 2661).
  • SC.2.SL.1.1, clarification 4: excludes detailed genetic mechanisms and evolutionary processes (fl-k5.txt, around line 5410).
  • SC.2.SL.1.2, clarification 5: excludes detailed genetic inheritance and natural-selection mechanisms (fl-k5.txt, around line 6012).
  • SC.5.SL.1.5, clarification 6: excludes detailed genetic mechanisms, DNA, Punnett squares, and formal natural-selection models (fl-k5.txt, around line 6106). The same benchmark includes that traits supporting survival may become more common within populations over time (clarification 4), so the exclusion is specifically about formal/detailed mechanisms.
  • SC.5.P.1.4, clarification 3: excludes evolutionary mechanisms while the surrounding benchmark covers plant structures, adaptations, and survival in different environments (fl-k5.txt, around line 7212).

Those limits are grade-band-specific and largely worded as limits on advanced/detailed/formal mechanisms. They should be distinguished from eliminating evolution instruction. The grade 6–8 draft explicitly teaches natural selection, inherited variation, overproduction, differential survival/reproduction, fossil evidence, and changes in life over time (including SC.8.SL.2.1–2.4 and SC.8.SL.3.2; fl-68.txt, around lines 6206–6480). Grades 9–12 coded statements include population variation/change, mutations, adaptation, fossils, and evidence-based revision of explanations (e.g. SC.912.SL.1.6 and SC.912.SL.2.7). Plant and vertebrate clarifications discuss evolutionary or phylogenetic relationships; those appear in supporting text rather than coded standard descriptions or numbered benchmark statements.

The 2008 adopted standards were more explicit in their organizing language: Big Idea 15 called evolution “the organizing principle of life science” and said it was supported by multiple forms of evidence; high-school Standard 15 called it “the fundamental concept underlying all of biology” and listed fossil, anatomical, embryological, biogeographic, molecular, and observed-change evidence (sources/2008/florida-2008-science-standards.txt, around lines 2123–2129 and 3371–3382). The 2008 text also had a separate Nature of Science statement distinguishing scientific questions from those addressed by art, philosophy, and religion (sources/2008/...txt, around lines 2688–2707). The first-round draft did not repeat that religion wording or the same “organizing/fundamental concept” formulation, while retaining natural-selection, population-change, adaptation, and fossil-related content. The October 26 draft adds a coded benchmark on evolutionary processes for marine-organism diversity and a clarification naming common ancestry. It does not repeat the 2008 cross-life organizing formulation in a coded benchmark; this is a placement difference, not an absence of all evolutionary wording.

Common descent and the antievolution claim landscape

The October 26 draft does name common ancestry in one benchmark clarification, attached to an expectation about marine-organism diversity, adaptation, and distribution. The phrase does not appear as a cross-life organizing statement in a coded benchmark. Earlier wording that said the current draft never named common ancestry was incorrect. The 2008 standards’ broader organizing statement remains a difference in scope and placement, but the exact ID/text crosswalk and bounded review of changed/new sentences are complete; the latter found continuity or partial matches in most items, with 11 unresolved. This does not establish a complete conceptual equivalence crosswalk. This is not a literal deletion of a sentence using “common descent” from the 2008 standards; that exact phrase was not located in the 2008 text either.

One interpretation is that leaving the cross-life organizing formulation out of coded benchmarks may be consequential in a political context where shared ancestry is a recurring target of antievolution advocacy. That interpretation is not evidence of the 2026 writers’ rationale or the beliefs of people commenting on the draft. The TalkOrigins Index to Creationist Claims illustrates recurring advocacy claims and frames, including arguments that evolution is “only a theory” or unfalsifiable (CA201, CA211), appeals to purported scientific rejection and quote-mining (CA111–CA113), denials of macroevolution and a micro/macro split (CB901, CB902), and demands that fossil evidence show direct ancestors rather than branching and side-lineage patterns (CC202). It also records “equal time,” “teach the controversy,” and teaching biology without evolution (CA040–CA042), alongside Intelligent Design arguments and their relation to creationism (CI001.1, CI001.2). We have not verified the Index’s total number of claim routes, and this list does not measure public opinion or establish influence on Florida’s draft.

The text comparison supports a narrower claim: the second-round draft names evolutionary processes in a marine-life benchmark and common ancestry in that benchmark’s clarification, but does not restate evolution as the organizing principle of all life science in a coded benchmark or retain the 2008 human-evolution outcomes. The implications for teaching are an interpretation, not a demonstrated classroom outcome. The National Academies’ framework connects common ancestry, converging evidence, natural selection, and speciation as core ideas. The draft’s placement could affect how explicitly students are required to synthesize them; this comparison alone cannot show what any classroom will teach. National Academies Framework, Chapter 6.

It also raises a teaching-integrity concern independent of intent. The National Academies’ framework organizes biological evolution around unity and diversity and identifies common ancestry, converging evidence, natural selection, and speciation as connected ideas. Florida’s draft names common ancestry in a clarification about marine organisms, but does not state a coded, cross-life synthesis or retain the 2008 human-evolution outcomes. That narrower scope could shift some work of connecting evidence and explanation to teachers and local materials; this is a curricular risk, not a measured classroom outcome. National Academies Framework, Chapter 6.

Climate and environmental content

The 2026 draft contains a dedicated grades 9–12 sequence on climate regulation: SC.912.PE.13.1–13.6 addresses radiation balance, greenhouse processes, atmospheric circulation, ocean circulation, feedback, and synthesis (see fl-912.txt, lines 6471–6612). The next sequence, SC.912.PE.16.1–16.7, addresses anthropogenic activities across Earth systems, evaluates evidence on atmospheric and energy-related processes, and evaluates sustainability/resilience strategies (lines 7032–7240). Thus the draft is not silent on human influences on climate or Earth systems. A separate animal-impact benchmark, SC.912.A.12.8, includes climate change among negative impacts on mammals (around line 17055); that isolated mention should not be used as a proxy for the more extensive PE.13 and PE.16 sequences.

The phrase “multiple interacting natural and anthropogenic factors” in SC.912.PE.16.2 is a normal description of complex Earth-system processes, not by itself false balance. The draft pairs it with long-term data analysis and evidence evaluation. No claim was found that the climate record is unreliable, that experts are divided, or that anthropogenic effects are unsupported.

That rhetoric finding does not resolve the separate omission question. Florida’s SC.912.PE.16.2 does require students to evaluate evidence about anthropogenic interactions with atmospheric and energy-related processes. What is not explicit is the full task of identifying the causes of climate change over the past century (MS-ESS3-5) and using climate-model outputs to forecast rates and impacts (HS-ESS3-5). The U.S. Global Change Research Program’s 2024 Climate Literacy Guide likewise treats observation, modeling, natural variability, and human-caused recent change as connected. Florida’s draft contains many component topics, so this is a risk that students may not be required to connect them—not evidence of a denialist statement. Reliance on uncoordinated local supplementation could also make that synthesis uneven.

Ambiguous scope limits worth tracking separately

Three passages explicitly fence off policy advocacy or policy analysis:

  • SC.5.EN.1.3, clarification 1 limits instruction to introductory energy-source comparisons and excludes efficiency calculations, electrical generation systems, and “energy policy analysis” (fl-k5.txt, around lines 620–640).
  • SC.8.NT.1.4, clarification 3 permits descriptive, evidence-based study of environmental and societal influences on nutrition while excluding “policy advocacy or prescriptive solutions” (fl-68.txt, around lines 10300–10470).
  • SC.6.ASL.1.1, clarification 4 excludes advanced ecological modeling or “policy advocacy,” though the benchmark itself has students analyze ecosystem data and propose evidence-based solutions (fl-68.txt, around lines 11440–11570).

These exclusions are not politically neutral in curricular effect. They draw a line between examining evidence about energy, nutrition, and ecosystems and evaluating policy choices or advocating prescriptive action. Students can reasonably take from that line that proposing public responses—including regulation of industry—is outside the legitimate work of science, even when they are asked to analyze evidence about environmental and societal effects. That curricular boundary aligns with political positions that government should have little or no role in regulating industry; it therefore warrants analysis as substantive framing, not treatment as a merely technical scope limit.

The pattern is selective rather than document-wide. SC.6.ASL.1.1 still asks students to propose evidence-based ecosystem solutions. In grades 9–12, other clarifications include safety, regulation, ethical considerations, sustainability, and risk management for microbial technologies; the climate and Earth-systems sequence also asks students to evaluate sustainability and resilience strategies. These counterexamples do not erase the specific exclusions in energy, nutrition, and ecology. They make the placement of the prohibitions more important to examine: the draft allows applied solutions and regulatory considerations in some settings while expressly removing policy analysis, advocacy, or prescription in others.

The 2008 text search found no direct counterpart to these exact exclusion formulations. That is a textual observation, not proof that the earlier standards required policy advocacy. The 2026 wording supports a finding about curricular boundaries and a plausible effect on what students are encouraged to consider. It does not, on its own, establish the drafters’ political intent or how teachers and districts will implement the limits.

Method and limitations

I reviewed all five packet extracts, including the foreword and supplemental tables, using lexical searches for sectarian terms; claims of expert disagreement, scientific agendas or distrust; anti-method language; and terms for uncertainty, evidence, peer review, consensus, evolution, and climate. I then read the surrounding text for the substantive hits, including the K–5 evolutionary-process exclusions, grades 8–12 evolution passages, Earth-interior uncertainty, climate evidence, scientific consensus, and policy-advocacy exclusions. The 2008 adopted standards were checked for direct comparison.

Keyword searches can miss coded or indirect framing, and a text-only review cannot determine how instructional exclusions would be implemented. Conversely, a subject-specific scope limitation, ordinary scientific uncertainty, or missing sentence is not enough to infer intent. No separate external content source or author was inferred from these findings.

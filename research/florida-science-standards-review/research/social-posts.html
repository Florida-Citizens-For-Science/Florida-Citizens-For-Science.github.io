The central concern is not just which scientific terms the draft uses. It is which explanations and tasks Florida no longer explicitly requires students to learn.

What the draft leaves out

  • Evolution: None of the 1,512 numbered benchmarks uses the word “evolution.” The draft does not name common ancestry as the explanation uniting evidence about life, and it omits the 2008 high-school expectations on human ancestry and fossil hominids.
  • Climate change: The phrase appears once in supporting text and in no numbered benchmark. Climate mechanisms and human Earth-system impacts remain, but the draft does not explicitly require the 2008 task of relating Earth-system and astronomical factors to global climate change, or the century-scale attribution and climate-model forecasting tasks found in national standards.

These are omissions in explicit, required learning outcomes—not a claim that all related science has disappeared. See the full comparison and subject analysis.

Plain-language overview

Florida’s 2026 draft retains natural selection, fossil evidence, climate-system mechanisms, and human impacts on Earth. But it no longer names evolution in a numbered benchmark, does not ask students to connect evolutionary evidence through common ancestry, and drops two specific 2008 expectations about human evolution. It teaches many parts of climate science while no longer making “climate change” a numbered learning outcome or explicitly requiring some attribution and forecasting tasks. Those changes deserve direct answers from the state.

Florida’s history makes these omissions politically salient. Evolution wording was contested during adoption of the 2008 standards; later reports described climate-term restrictions under Rick Scott and climate-content edits requested for textbooks under Ron DeSantis. That history warrants scrutiny but does not prove why the 2026 workgroup made these choices. The historical evidence note and project bibliography document the sources and limits.

Evolution

Florida’s 2008 standards called evolution biology’s organizing principle. In the 2026 draft, none of 1,512 numbered benchmarks uses “evolution”; common ancestry is not named as the explanation connecting the evidence; and the human-ancestry and fossil-hominid expectations are gone. Natural selection and fossils remain. Read the comparison and source notes.

Human evolution

The 2008 Florida standards explicitly asked high-school students to examine human ancestry and what fossil hominids show about human evolution. No equivalent expectation appears in the 2026 draft. That is a specific content omission, even though other evolution-related material remains. See the evidence.

Climate change

Florida’s 2008 standards asked students to relate Earth-system and astronomical factors to global climate change. The 2026 draft retains climate mechanisms and human Earth-system impacts, but “climate change” appears once only in a clarification—not a benchmark—and those explicit attribution/forecasting tasks are missing. This is a gap in required synthesis, not the disappearance of all climate science. Read the detailed crosswalk.

Why the history matters

Florida adopted evolution in 2008 after a contested last-minute wording change. Later reporting described climate-term restrictions under Rick Scott and state-requested climate edits to textbooks under Ron DeSantis. That record makes today’s evolution and climate omissions politically consequential; it does not prove the 2026 workgroup’s motive. Read the verified chronology.

Process

The public draft PDFs do not include successive drafts, tracked changes, meeting minutes, or comment dispositions. Those records are needed to explain how the final draft came to omit or reframe explicit evolution and climate-change expectations—and to determine when those decisions occurred and why. See the records request.

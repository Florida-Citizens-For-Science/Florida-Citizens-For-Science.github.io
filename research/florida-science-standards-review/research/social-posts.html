No “new code” is treated as a new concept. The separate STARS forms also remain under review. Read the roadmap.

What is named, and where

  • Evolution-related wording: The draft has no separately named evolution standard or topic. One high-school benchmark uses the phrase “evolutionary processes” for marine organisms; its clarification includes common ancestry. The coded requirement is limited to marine-life diversity, adaptation, and distribution. A targeted search did not locate equivalents of the 2008 human-ancestry and fossil-hominid outcomes; the exact ID/text diff is complete; a bounded review of changed/new statements finds mostly continuity or partial matches, with some task-specificity gaps and 11 unresolved rows.
  • Climate change: The phrase appears once in a benchmark clarification, not in a coded standard or numbered benchmark. The phrase appeared in four 2008 benchmark statements. Grades 8 and 9–12 include evidence-analysis tasks about human and natural climate influences. The narrower task difference is that the draft does not state the NGSS past-century attribution and global-model forecasting tasks in the same explicit form. See the climate task audit.
  • Earth and universe ages: Supporting guidance gives Earth’s age as approximately 4 billion years and the universe’s age as approximately 13.8 billion years. These are present in the second-round proposal, though not in the coded benchmark sentences. “Big Bang” is not named in the three grade-band PDFs.

These are differences in scope and placement across layers—not a claim that all related science has disappeared. The FDOE foreword says clarifications and examples help define expected outcomes and “carry the full weight of the standards”; final legal status depends on the adopted rule and files. Plain-language overview

Florida’s October 26 second-round draft does not give evolution a separately named standard or topic. One marine-life benchmark uses the phrase “evolutionary processes,” and its clarification includes common ancestry. A targeted search did not locate equivalents of two 2008 human-evolution outcomes; the exact ID/text diff is complete; a bounded review of changed/new statements finds mostly continuity or partial matches, with some task-specificity gaps and 11 unresolved rows. “Climate change” appears once in a clarification, not a coded benchmark; a task-level climate comparison is complete; it finds specific differences in task framing, not absence of human-influence content. The FDOE foreword says supporting clarifications and examples carry the full weight of expected outcomes, so their placement matters too. Florida should explain how these pieces fit together.

Florida’s history makes these choices politically salient. Evolution wording was contested during adoption of the 2008 standards; later reports described climate-term restrictions under Rick Scott and climate-content edits requested for textbooks under Ron DeSantis. That history warrants scrutiny but does not prove why the 2026 workgroup made these choices. The historical evidence note and project bibliography document the sources and limits.

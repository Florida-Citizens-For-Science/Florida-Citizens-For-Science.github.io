The central concern is not just which scientific terms the draft uses. It is which explanations and tasks Florida no longer explicitly requires students to learn.

Scope note: “1,512 benchmarks” refers to the three grade-band PDFs. The FDOE Forms contain 11 numbered K–12 STARS standards absent from those PDFs and the current register. All 26 forms now have visible-text captures (25 complete, one partial); the 1,512 count remains limited to PDF leaf benchmarks. Read the roadmap.

What the draft leaves out

  • Evolution: No numbered benchmark uses “evolution” biologically; a parent standard uses “landscape evolution” in the geological sense. The draft does not name common ancestry as the explanation uniting evidence about life, and it omits the 2008 high-school expectations on human ancestry and fossil hominids.
  • Climate change: The phrase appears once in a benchmark clarification, not in a coded standard or numbered benchmark. Climate mechanisms and human Earth-system impacts remain, but the draft does not explicitly require the 2008 task of relating Earth-system and astronomical factors to global climate change, or the century-scale attribution and climate-model forecasting tasks found in national standards.

These are omissions in explicit, required learning outcomes—not a claim that all related science has disappeared. See the full comparison and subject analysis.

Plain-language overview

Florida’s 2026 draft retains natural selection, fossil evidence, climate-system mechanisms, and human impacts on Earth. But no coded parent or leaf statement names biological evolution or asks students to connect evolutionary evidence through common ancestry (the phrase “landscape evolution” appears in one parent standard in a geologic sense), and drops two specific 2008 expectations about human evolution. It teaches many parts of climate science while no longer making “climate change” a numbered learning outcome or explicitly requiring some attribution and forecasting tasks. Those changes deserve direct answers from the state.

Florida’s history makes these omissions politically salient. Evolution wording was contested during adoption of the 2008 standards; later reports described climate-term restrictions under Rick Scott and climate-content edits requested for textbooks under Ron DeSantis. That history warrants scrutiny but does not prove why the 2026 workgroup made these choices. The historical evidence note and project bibliography document the sources and limits.

Evolution

Florida’s 2008 standards called evolution biology’s organizing principle. In the 2026 draft, no numbered benchmark uses “evolution” biologically (a parent standard says “landscape evolution” geologically); common ancestry is not named as the explanation connecting the evidence; and the human-ancestry and fossil-hominid expectations are gone. Natural selection and fossils remain. Read the comparison and source notes.

Human evolution

The 2008 Florida standards explicitly asked high-school students to examine human ancestry and what fossil hominids show about human evolution. No equivalent expectation appears in the 2026 draft. That is a specific content omission, even though other evolution-related material remains. See the evidence.

Climate change

Florida’s 2008 standards asked students to relate Earth-system and astronomical factors to global climate change. The 2026 draft retains climate mechanisms and human Earth-system impacts, but “climate change” appears once in a clarification—not in coded standard or benchmark text—and some explicit attribution/forecasting tasks are missing. This is a gap in required synthesis, not the disappearance of all climate science. Read the detailed crosswalk.

Why the history matters

Florida adopted evolution in 2008 after a contested last-minute wording change. Later reporting described climate-term restrictions under Rick Scott and state-requested climate edits to textbooks under Ron DeSantis. That record makes today’s evolution and climate omissions politically consequential; it does not prove the 2026 workgroup’s motive. Read the verified chronology.

Franklin Standards comparison

A LinkedIn article says Florida’s draft resembles the 2024 Franklin Standards. We compared the source: Franklin explicitly names evolution, climate change, Earth’s age, and the universe’s age. Florida retains related science but states some of those expectations less explicitly. Our text screen found no distinctive extended wording match, and no record establishes that Florida copied or adopted Franklin. Read the comparison and limits.

Process

The public draft PDFs do not include successive drafts, tracked changes, meeting minutes, or comment dispositions. Those records are needed to explain how the final draft came to omit or reframe explicit evolution and climate-change expectations—and to determine when those decisions occurred and why. See the records request.

College preparation

FDOE says this rewrite should prepare students for college-level science. Do the draft’s required outcomes line up? A lack of a located university statement after five weeks tells us little; silence is not assent. Ask FDOE and Florida universities for a standards-to-entry-course crosswalk. UC’s 2008 case is a narrow course-approval precedent, not Florida credit rejection. Read the evidence and limits.

Earth’s age and the universe’s origin

Florida’s 2008 standards asked students how scientists measure Earth’s age and named the Big Bang as the universe’s origin. The 2026 draft retains dating methods and evidence for cosmic expansion, but does not ask students to state Earth’s age or name the Big Bang. A contemporary Franklin model explicitly gives both ages; Florida’s draft retains dating and cosmic-expansion evidence but omits those age statements. That is a specific scope omission, not an absence of geology or cosmology. See the source-first comparison.

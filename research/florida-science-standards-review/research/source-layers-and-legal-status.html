What is a standard, and what is supporting material?

The FDOE review portal presents several kinds of text together. They are not interchangeable evidence of what students will be required to learn. This page records the source and rulemaking distinctions used throughout the review.

Version status: This page’s row-level classifications and derived counts were produced against the first-round 2026 draft. FDOE’s second-round PDFs, dated October 26, contain 1,572 unique leaf benchmark IDs (279 K–5, 235 grades 6–8, 1,058 grades 9–12) and 339 parent standards. Adoption status comes first

Florida Statutes §1003.41 says state academic standards establish the core content of curricula and specify the knowledge and skills K–12 public-school students are expected to acquire. It also says new and revised standards documents submitted to the State Board for approval must consist only of academic standards, and sets July 1, 2026 as the submission date for revised standards documents. The latest adopted record for Rule 6A-1.09401, effective August 3, 2026, lists Florida’s 2016 Science Standards (Ref-06754) and 2016 Science Access Points (Ref-06469); the 2026 FDOE files remain labeled review-only and non-final. The October 14 rule-development notice says newly revised science standards identified during review will be considered but lists no reference materials. As of October 29, the 2026 draft has not been adopted as the state rule. The timeline record alone does not establish what was submitted by the statutory date or whether any later action changed the schedule; this review does not assess compliance. Read §1003.41 · Current adopted Rule 6A-1.09401 record and incorporated references · October 14 rule-development notice · FDOE review portal.

Legal boundary: a sentence in a review PDF is not a current statutory requirement merely because it is on the FDOE site or in the same file as a benchmark. Conversely, this review does not label every clarification or example “nonbinding”: the legal effect after adoption depends on the exact standard document and materials incorporated into the final rule. The FDOE science portal itself says its Florida-specific examples provide context rather than separate content expectations; that statement applies to those contextual examples and does not settle every embedded clarification or task. FDOE’s 2026–27 computer-science adoption specifications, by contrast, explicitly say clarifications and examples are adopted as part of those computer-science standards and set a baseline for mastery. This subject-specific example shows why the final science rule and incorporated file must be checked directly. Read the computer-science specifications.

What the draft says about instructional expectations

The proposed science foreword makes a clear instructional claim: benchmarks, clarifications, and examples together “represent the expected outcomes” and “carry the full weight of the standards.” It also says benchmarks should be integrated rather than treated as isolated units, and that related benchmarks should be mastered by the end of a grade or course. Those statements mean clarifications cannot be dismissed as optional filler when assessing the draft’s intended scope or the work teachers may need to do. They do not, by themselves, settle the legal effect of every clarification after adoption; that still depends on the final rule and its incorporated materials. Read the proposed foreword.

This distinction matters for workload claims. Dr. Victor Sampson’s October 6, 2026 article counts 3,935 high-school clarifications and models the time needed to cover them. The count is a useful signal of the amount of benchmark-linked guidance, but its “24 credits to introduce” and “73 credits to mastery” figures are scenarios, not measured time requirements. They assume each clarification is one discrete topic, allot one or three 50-minute periods to each, and assume that every student must master every grade-9–12 item within only the three graduation-required science credits. The foreword’s integration principle directly challenges the one-clarification/one-topic assumption; the statutory three-credit minimum does not establish that every grade-band expectation is assigned to those three courses. The article usefully identifies a question the state should answer with a course-to-benchmark map, actual schedules, and teacher-tested pacing estimates. Until those exist, this review does not claim that Florida’s draft is too large to teach. See the bibliography entry and source note.

What the word counts now mean

An earlier first-round leaf-only word comparison reported a small difference between Florida 2026 and 2008, but this verification found a two-word text-boundary artifact in one 2026 register row. An independent extraction of parent descriptions also failed to reproduce the published total. We withdraw the exact formal-text totals and percentages until the parsing method and source boundaries can be reproduced, then rerun them on the October 26 PDFs. These figures must not be used as current draft size claims. The source-layer count file records this verification status and the first-round packet method.

As a historical whole-file measure, the first-round grade-band PDFs contain 216,632 whitespace-separated tokens in the preserved PyMuPDF extracts; the separate foreword and tables add 23,039, for a five-PDF total of 239,671. These counts include coded wording, supporting guidance, examples, tasks, headings, repeated labels, and layout text. The audit did not retain a reproducible segmentation for the portion outside coded text, so no such subtotal is reported. Packet totals describe the first-round files only; they are not adopted-standard counts and should not be compared directly with the 2008 PDF as though the packages had the same structure.

Method: leaf benchmark wording was counted from the first-round benchmark registers; 2026 parent descriptions were extracted from the three grade-band text files by parent-level standard code; 2008 parent clauses were extracted from lettered A–D statements under Big Idea/Standard headings. Counts use a Unicode-aware \b\w+\b token pattern and exclude IDs, titles, and the associated clarifications/examples/tasks. The source files and extracts are in the private dated corpus; the full comparison gives the limitations.

How this changes the omission test

For a claim that an expectation is absent, reviewers search the coded standard descriptions and numbered benchmark statements first. Supporting text is then checked and reported in its own category. It can show what FDOE proposed as context for instruction, but the review will not use that material to claim a core standard explicitly requires an idea unless the final adopted reference gives it that status. This distinction is particularly important where a concept appears only in a clarification or sample task.

The first-round lexical findings below predate the October 26 second-round PDFs and should not be read as current findings. The second-round PDFs contain one coded high-school benchmark (SC.912.PE.18.1) naming evolutionary processes for marine organisms; its clarification names common ancestry. The phrase “climate change” appears once in a benchmark clarification (SC.912.A.12.8), not in coded parent or leaf text. A grade-7 supporting task and clarification give Earth’s age as approximately 4 billion years (SC.7.PE.3.2); a grade-6 clarification gives the universe’s age as approximately 13.8 billion years (SC.6.SP.1.2). The phrase “Big Bang” does not appear in the second-round PDFs. These are text-placement findings, not a complete cross-version equivalence analysis; the full October 26 crosswalk remains pending.

Design supplements for the adopted boundary

Because the FDOE packet comes from the state education agency, teachers and districts may treat its clarifications, examples, or tables as more authoritative than a third-party supplement, even where the final rule does not incorporate those materials. That is a plausible implementation barrier, not a measured outcome. A useful supplement should map each lesson to adopted code language, label which details come from adopted standards and which add evidence-based instruction, and avoid relying on a review-only clarification as the sole place a core idea appears. Educators and district curriculum staff should test usability before a large investment.

The removal scenario is also a specific monitoring question: if the public-review packet uses a clarification or example to signal a concept, but the adopted reference omits that text, then the review packet may have suggested broader coverage than the operative standard requires. Preserve the dated draft, proposed rule, final rule, and every incorporated file with filenames and hashes; compare the same text layers and report the exact changes and effective date. That is a document-version risk to watch. Without separate evidence, it does not establish an intentional “ploy.”

Track the adoption boundary, not presumed intent

The review packet can create an impression that a concept is covered because a clarification or task mentions it, even when the coded standard-and-benchmark text does not state that expectation. If the adopted reference omits that support text, the final operative standard could be narrower than the public-review packet suggested. This is the specific post-adoption version risk raised in the review; the current record does not establish that FDOE intends such a change. At each rulemaking milestone, preserve and compare the exact text of the proposed rule, every incorporated reference, the adopted rule, and the final standards files. Report what changed and when, then assess motive only if separate evidence supports it.

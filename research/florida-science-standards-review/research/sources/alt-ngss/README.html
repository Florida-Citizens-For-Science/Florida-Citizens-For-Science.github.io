NGSS and federal framework comparison notes

Scope: a first-pass exact and near-exact wording comparison of the 2026 Florida draft against the official NGSS topic-arranged standards (2017 PDF), NGSS middle-school life-science evidence statements (2015 PDF), the NGSS appendix on progressions (2013), and an official National Academies report brief about the 2012 NRC Framework. Draft text was read from the K–5, 6–8, and 9–12 extracted files in ../2026/.

Strong near-verbatim parallels in NGSS Topic Arrangements

1. The Sun’s relative brightness: likely text transfer

  • Florida draft: K–5 extracted text, fl-k5.txt:4390–4391, clarification under SC.2.SP.1.2: “the Sun is a star that appears brighter than other stars because it is much closer to Earth.”
  • NGSS: Topic Arrangements PDF, p. 31, DCI ESS1.A at 5-ESS1-1: “The sun is a star that appears larger and brighter than other stars because it is closer. Stars range greatly in their distance from Earth.”
  • Assessment: a distinctive clause with essentially the same syntax, causal explanation, and comparison set; Florida drops “larger,” adds “much,” and makes the Earth reference explicit. This is a strong near-verbatim borrowing candidate, beyond shared scientific content. It is the strongest result in this pass.

2. Ecosystem components and populations: likely text transfer

  • Florida draft: 6–8 extracted text, fl-68.txt:6054–6055, SC.8.SL.1.3: “Analyze how disruptions to physical or biological components of ecosystems affect populations.”
  • NGSS: Topic Arrangements PDF, p. 47, MS-LS2-4: “Construct an argument supported by empirical evidence that changes to physical or biological components of an ecosystem affect populations.”
  • Assessment: the uncommon sequence “physical or biological components of [an] ecosystem affect populations” is retained essentially intact; Florida changes “changes” to “disruptions,” pluralizes “ecosystem,” and changes the student operation. Same middle-school ecosystem passage and causal relationship. Strong near-verbatim borrowing candidate, though NGSS itself could have been mediated by another document.

Independent predecessor match: Florida 2008

  • Florida draft: sources/2026/fl-68.txt:5882–5883, SC.7.SL.2.1: “Explain how populations change over time as a result of births, deaths, immigration, emigration, and limiting factors.”
  • Florida 2008 standard SC.912.L.17.5 (PDF printed p. 71): “Analyze how population size is determined by births, deaths, immigration, emigration, and limiting factors (biotic and abiotic) that determine carrying capacity.” The 2009 FDOE science specifications and Biology 1 EOC item specifications reproduce this text: https://www.fldoe.org/core/fileparse.php/7535/urlt/sciencespecs2009.pdf; https://cdn.fldoe.org/core/fileparse.php/5662/urlt/SCIBIO1EOCTSI.pdf.
  • Match: the seven-word contiguous sequence “births, deaths, immigration, emigration, and limiting factors” appears verbatim in both; the draft keeps the same ordered list and makes the rest into a grade 7 explanation standard. This is a strong wording lineage candidate and a second, pre-NGSS Florida source family. Caution: the ecological population-growth model is standard disciplinary content; sequence, same state, and code/content continuity raise the match above generic concept similarity, but do not identify which 2008-era or later intermediary the writers consulted.

This is now two distinct source families with near-verbatim or exact wording in the 2026 draft: (a) NGSS/NRC lineage (two passages above) and (b) Florida’s own pre-NGSS 2008 standard lineage (population benchmark above). Under the literal user stop rule, recommend stopping source collection now. This does not establish independent copying from NGSS vs MA, nor attribution or authorization; MA is only an alternative copy of NGSS wording.

Candidate echo, downgraded

The 2015 MS-LS4-6 evidence statement says “evidence that distributions of traits in populations change over time in response to changes in environmental conditions” (PDF p. 34). Draft SC.8.SL.2.3 asks students to identify how a population’s trait frequency “changes over time in response to changes in water clarity and seagrass coverage” and then explain which traits become more common through natural selection. This is close in syntax and task structure, but the shared language is partly standard disciplinary phrasing; the Florida example supplies distinct local content. Treat as a plausible task-template influence, not a verified lift.

The 2015 evidence statement also contains “that all living things are made of cells”; Florida uses “all living things are made of cells” in fl-912.txt:9921 and elsewhere. This short, basic scientific proposition is generic, so it is not counted as a lift.

Massachusetts 2016 standard 5-ESS1-1 has the same Sun sentence as NGSS. The Massachusetts DESE FAQ says its standard labels are based on NGSS and that Massachusetts adapted the NGSS standards; CA also explicitly adopted NGSS and aligned its framework to it. Accordingly, these are not counted as independent origin sources for the sentence. The Massachusetts PDF itself is preserved here to document that test: https://www.doe.mass.edu/frameworks/scitech/2016-04.pdf, physical PDF p. 51 / extracted text around line 1995.

Method and limits

I normalized case, punctuation, and line breaks and searched for common token runs (including runs of eight or more tokens), then checked the draft and source passages side by side. Exact matching did not find longer runs in the NGSS set except standard instructional stems such as “Develop and use a model to describe.” Such stems are excluded as boilerplate. Near matches above were manually checked against both source and draft text and assessed for phrase distinctiveness and grade/content alignment. This was a bounded NGSS/federal-framework sample, not a global source search, and absence of a match in this sample does not rule out another intermediary.

Deep crosswalk pass: high-school plant science

Review date: October 22, 2026
Scope: The 83 unresolved 2026 high-school SC.912.P.* benchmark rows present at the start of this pass. In this draft’s taxonomy, P is the plant-science strand; these rows concern plant cell biology, physiology, ecology, and human uses, not physics. The set was compared directly with the Florida 2008 high-school standards and the surviving 1996 high-school standards. The 1998/99 GLE documents cover K–8, so they were checked for context but not used as high-school predecessor matches.

Results

This pass adds 80 concept-level matches: 7 high-confidence matches for plant-group classification and 73 medium-confidence matches for broader predecessor concepts. Three rows remain unresolved after direct review. Seven additional rows were mapped on second, source-specific reviews; the match notes identify what the older wording supports and which specific plant structures, mechanisms, data tasks, or applications the draft adds.

The strongest 2008 anchors are SC.912.L.14.2–.3 for plant and animal cell components, structure/function, and membrane transport; SC.912.L.14.8–.9 for plant life cycles and the relationships between plant organs/tissues and physiological processes; and SC.912.L.14.53 for plant classification, including bryophytes, pteridophytes, gymnosperms, and angiosperms. The predecessor also covers reproduction, natural selection and variation, succession, population limits, organism relationships, food webs and decomposers, invasive non-native species, and water/carbon/nitrogen cycles. These support continuity at the concept-family level. The draft often specifies named organelles, particular plant mechanisms, measurement procedures, or applications that the older benchmark does not name.

The 1996 high-school material supplies broader earlier parallels: cell processes and membranes (SC.F.1.4, Standard 1); sexual and asexual reproduction, adaptation, and genetic variation (SC.F.2.4, Standard 2); organism interdependence and ecosystem energy flow (SC.G.1.4, Standard 1); and environmental limits on productivity and adaptation (SC.G.2.4, Standard 2). It does not provide an earlier plant-specific inventory of organelles, plant tissues, plant hormones, or plant ecological services. The 1998/99 K–8 GLEs include general plant-cell, plant-part, reproduction, and ecosystem concepts. Where a GLE link is used for a high-school leaf, the register labels it as cross-grade continuity, not same-grade high-school coverage.

The three unresolved rows concern plant-hormone regulation of growth and reproduction and plant-derived medicine/health applications. A second review found partial predecessors for integrated plant organ/physiology relationships, invasive-species management, pollution effects, plant support for human survival, and ecosystem-service components; each leaves the draft’s integration, plant-specific strategies, human-benefit synthesis, physiology, and data tasks as added detail. The 2008 resource benchmark also names forests among resources for cost-benefit evaluation, giving a limited predecessor for the draft’s economic and societal importance benchmark; its note explicitly bounds that match. Unresolved rows are unresolved against the reviewed source corpus, not findings that the concepts are new or absent from instruction.

SC.912.P.9.8 — analyze plants as integrated biological systems 2008 SC.912.L.14.8, lines 3206–3208, relates structures of major plant organs and tissues to physiological processes. Medium, partial. The draft builds a broader whole-plant model linking matter and energy flows, water/mineral transport, photosynthesis, respiration, growth, reproduction, environmental feedback, and homeostasis; the predecessor does not state this integration/modeling task.
SC.912.P.19.1 — explain how plants support human survival/well-being 1998/99 GLE SC.G.1.1.2, lines 584–593, says plants produce oxygen and food for animals and describes plant/animal interdependence; 2008 SC.3.L.14.1 and .17.2, lines 1354 and 1392, cover plant food production, water/nutrient transport, and photosynthesis; high-school 2008 .17.9–.10 and .17.19, lines 3577–3584 and 3618–3623, cover ecosystem cycles and human use/renewal of resources. Medium, partial, with grade movement. These sources support selected plant contributions to food, oxygen, and resources. The grade-1 GLE and grade-3 standards are well below the draft high-school grade band. The draft integrates nutrition, atmospheric gases, water quality, soil stability, and human-population sustainability into one human-well-being account; that synthesis is not stated in the predecessors.
SC.912.P.19.2 — explain plant ecosystem services benefiting human populations 2008 SC.912.L.17.9–.10, lines 3577–3584, covers ecosystem cycles and resource costs/benefits including water and forests; .17.12, lines 3594–3597, addresses sustainable land use; .17.19, lines 3618–3623, addresses resource production, renewal, and availability. Medium, partial. The sources support several components of provisioning, regulating, and supporting services. They do not name the ecosystem-services framework or integrate the functions as benefits to human populations and environmental stability; that synthesis and the explicit service categories are draft additions.
SC.912.P.12.6 — calculate net primary production 1996 SC.G.2.4, lines 127 and 144–145, identifies physical factors limiting ecosystem productivity; 2008 SC.912.L.17.8, lines 3573–3576, describes energy transfer through food webs and trophic levels. Medium, partial. These support ecosystem productivity and energy flow, but neither defines or calculates net primary production (GPP minus plant respiration). The formula, calculation, and interpretation remain draft-specific in the reviewed corpus.
SC.912.P.18.3 — analyze human-caused hydrologic changes that affect plants 2008 SC.7.E.6.6, lines 2237–2238, names human changes to water flow; SC.3.L.17.2, line 1392, says plants use water to make food; SC.912.E.7.7, lines 2912–2915, addresses hydrologic conditions affecting people. The 1998/99 GLE SC.G.1.2.3, lines 757–769, links water use with plant growth, maintenance, and reproduction. Medium, partial, with substantial grade movement. The sources separately support human changes to water flow and plant water-dependence, but no predecessor connects human hydrologic change to plant growth, distribution, or reproduction. That causal integration and the named examples (irrigation, diversion, drainage, groundwater withdrawal, and land-cover change) are additions in the draft.

Method and limits

All 83 previously unresolved rows were read and compared against the relevant 2008 and 1996 high-school text; the preserved 1998/99 GLEs were checked for earlier cross-grade exposure. Broad retrieval similarity alone was not treated as a match. Where a predecessor speaks generally about cell components, plant anatomy, reproduction, or ecosystems, the register records that limited concept-level connection and explicitly notes details not stated. Where no source-supported connection was strong enough, the row remains unresolved. No divergence or non-divergence was inferred from failure to retrieve a predecessor.

The 2008 line locators in the register were checked against physical LF line numbering in sources/2008/florida-2008-science-standards.txt. The main 2008 anchors for this pass are SC.912.L.14.2 (line 3186), .14.3 (3190), .14.8 (3206), .14.9 (3209), .14.53 (3369), .15.13–.15 (3433, 3437, 3440), .16.14, .16.16–.17 (3515, 3522, 3526), and .17.4–.11 (3557, 3561, 3565, 3573, 3577, 3581, 3585). The 1996 comparison was checked against sources/1996/florida-1996-science-912.txt, especially lines 124–160 and 169–178. The exact draft rows, source locators, and scope notes are in the benchmark register.

This is a source-based crosswalk, not evidence of who drafted or accessed the 2026 standards. It does not establish that a concept missing from these written standards is absent from classroom instruction.

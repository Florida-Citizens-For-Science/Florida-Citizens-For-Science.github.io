Historical context: why these placements matter

Florida’s 2008 standards adopted evolution after a contested last-minute wording change; the same adopted standards explicitly asked students to study global climate change. The adoption record reviewed here identifies an evolution wording dispute, not a comparable climate-language fight. Later reporting described climate-term restrictions in state agencies under Rick Scott and requested climate-content cuts to textbooks under Ron DeSantis. This history makes the 2026 loss of explicit evolution framing and benchmark-level climate-change language a central public question. It does not establish the current workgroup’s motive. See the verified evidence note and project bibliography.

1996 Sunshine State Standards Grades 6–8: survival characteristics and fossil-record evidence of change (SC.F.2.3.3–.4); adaptations enhancing reproductive success (SC.G.1.3.2). Grades 9–12: mechanisms of change including mutation and natural selection, adaptation and diversity (SC.F.2.4.3); genetic variation and selection (SC.G.1.4.3). Fossil evidence and adaptation are in middle grades; mechanisms of evolutionary change are explicit in high school. Non-divergent with the broad two-stage progression in the 2026 draft.

The 6th/7th GLE entries for fossil evidence explicitly defer content to grade 8. The GLE narrows placement inside the 1996 6–8 band. It does not specify a middle-school natural-selection mechanism in the reviewed 6–8 text. Diverges from 2008’s grade 7 natural-selection emphasis; aligns more closely with 2026’s grade 8 location for the explicit mechanism.
2008 NGSSS Grade 7: fossil evidence consistent with evolution (SC.7.L.15.1), genetic variation/environmental factors and natural selection (.15.2), and failure to adapt/extinction (.15.3), printed pp.48–49. High school: Big Idea 15 SC.912.L.15.1–.15, including evidence, speciation, Hardy–Weinberg, natural-selection conditions (.15.13) and other mechanisms (.15.14). Evolution mechanisms are named in grade 7 and revisited in a detailed high-school sequence. Diverges from 2026 by one grade for the explicit middle-school mechanism.
2026 review draft Grade 8: SC.8.SL.2.1–.2.4 covers selection conditions, inherited variation and survival/reproduction, population change over time, and natural vs artificial selection (draft pp.136–140); SC.8.SL.3.1–.3.2 addresses evidence and fossil-record change (pp.141–142). High school: SC.912.SL.6.1–.6.6 distributes variation, survival, reproductive success, population response, and explanations across a sequence (draft pp.199–203). Core selection concepts persist, with a shift from grade 7 in 2008 to grade 8 in 2026. The current draft gives grade 8 an explicit mechanism sequence and retains related population-change work in high school. “Natural selection” is explicit in the grade-8 strand; in the located high-school sequence it appears in a historical-development clarification (SC.912.SL.6.5 clarification 7), rather than as the named umbrella benchmark used in 2008. Diverges in grade placement and explicit explanatory scope; retained selection and population concepts do not make the full sequence equivalent.

Normalized takeaway: Compared with 2008, Florida moves the named middle-school natural-selection sequence from grade 7 to grade 8, while retaining fossil evidence and substantial high-school population-change content. Those retained elements do not erase the remaining differences. The October 26 draft includes a coded high-school benchmark, SC.912.PE.18.1, asking students to analyze how evolutionary processes contribute to the diversity, adaptation, and distribution of marine organisms. Its first clarification says: “Instruction includes natural selection, adaptation, speciation, extinction, and common ancestry” (9–12 PDF, p. 174). The draft therefore does name evolutionary processes in a coded benchmark and common ancestry in its clarification. It does not state evolution as the cross-life organizing principle used in the 2008 standards, nor did the bounded review locate equivalents of the 2008 high-school tasks about hominid ancestry and what hominid fossils show about human evolution (SC.912.L.15.10–.11). Neither the 2008 text nor the October 26 Florida text uses the exact phrase “common descent”; the comparison concerns scope, task, and placement, not a literal phrase deletion. The 2026 benchmarks also do not locate several 2008 tasks on molecular-clock dating, reproductive isolation/speciation, Hardy–Weinberg calculations, genetic drift, and gene flow. The National Academies’ framework treats evolution as the unity and diversity of life, supported by shared ancestry and multiple lines of evidence. Without explicit synthesis, students may be taught selection, fossils, and relatedness as disconnected facts, and the work of supplying a coherent account—including humans’ place in evolutionary history—falls to local curriculum and teachers. That is a risk to accurate, consistent instruction, not proof that every classroom will omit these connections. The narrower scope and placement may be sectarianly consequential given the documented history; this does not establish the drafters’ intent. See the National Academies Framework and the sectarian-context analysis.

Primary sources: October 26 Florida grade-band drafts are listed on the FDOE review portal; the 2008 adopted text is linked through the project bibliography. The NGSS comparator tasks are available at MS-ESS3-5 and the official high-school Weather and Climate topic arrangement.

Second-round normalized takeaway: The October 26 packet has substantial climate coverage: greenhouse processes, radiation balance, circulation, feedback, oscillations, ocean heat transport, climate-pattern change, human influence, and evidence/data analysis. Grade 8 explicitly compares natural processes and human influences on climate-pattern change; the high-school Earth-systems clarification calls for evaluation of long-term atmospheric composition, energy balance, temperature, and precipitation evidence while considering natural and anthropogenic factors and uncertainty. The strongest remaining differences are narrower task formulations: the NGSS asks students to evaluate evidence for the past-century global-temperature rise and major human contribution, and to use global climate-model results to forecast change rates and impacts. Florida’s current text includes component reasoning but does not state those integrated tasks in the same explicit form. Grade-8 weather prediction over 24–48 hours and regional climate-pattern prediction are present; they are not equivalent to long-range climate projection. “Climate change” appears once as an exact phrase (in a mammal-impact clarification) and “global warming” has no exact hit, but those lexical results alone say little about climate coverage.

First-round normalized takeaway (historical): The first-round task crosswalk found substantial climate coverage in regulation, mechanisms, and human Earth-system impacts, alongside task-level differences from Florida 2008 and NGSS. The October 26 draft changed relevant placements and tasks, so the first-round row dispositions should not be treated as current findings. The 2024 U.S. Climate Literacy Guide is a climate-literacy comparator, not evidence of Florida alignment. See also the instructional-integrity analysis.

Additional comparator: the 2024 Franklin Standards comparison provides a direct content comparison with a contemporaneous K–12 model. Florida’s October 26 draft includes approximate Earth and universe ages in supporting text, names evolutionary processes in a marine benchmark, and names common ancestry in its clarification. Climate change appears in one clarification. An eight-word text screen found no distinctive extended Florida–Franklin wording match; no evidence establishes that Florida adopted or copied the model. Corpus limits and source anchors

  • 1996: four surviving Florida standards PDFs cover PreK–2, 3–5, 6–8, and 9–12. The files are preserved scans/exports, not the complete original curriculum-framework publication. The 6–8 item is independently matched to an Internet Archive capture. Repository landing page: USF/FCIT Sunshine State Standards; archived 6–8 source: Internet Archive, science6.pdf.
  • 1998/99: retained files are Grade Level Expectations, not evidence of a separately adopted 1998 replacement standards set. The 6–8 GLE is cited by its printed page and benchmark ID; the unavailable high-school GLE prevents a complete vertical comparison. The contemporaneous 1998 review says the added grade expectations retained the 1996 benchmark statements; see ERIC ED439133.
  • 2008: local PDF is a mirror; FDOE confirms the adopted standards history at the official Florida science standards page. The benchmark citations above use the PDF’s printed page numbers, which differ by one from pdftotext page indexing in some locations.
  • 2026: draft is from FDOE’s standards review page. Page numbers above are PDF page indices matching the text extracts. The PDFs are drafts under review, not adopted standards.
  • There is no recovered full 2026 source DOCX/version history here. This appendix cannot establish who moved or revised the benchmarks, when those changes occurred, or which earlier text was used.

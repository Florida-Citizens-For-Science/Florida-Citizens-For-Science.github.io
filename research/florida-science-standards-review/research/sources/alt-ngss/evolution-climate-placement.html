2026 draft register and evolution/climate placement crosswalk

The source register was last cut off on October 22, 2026; this analysis was updated October 24. The comparison covers grade placement and content scope. It does not establish wording provenance.

Historical context: why these placements matter

Florida’s 2008 standards adopted evolution after a contested last-minute wording change; the same adopted standards explicitly asked students to study global climate change. The adoption record reviewed here identifies an evolution wording dispute, not a comparable climate-language fight. Later reporting described climate-term restrictions in state agencies under Rick Scott and requested climate-content cuts to textbooks under Ron DeSantis. This history makes the 2026 loss of explicit evolution framing and benchmark-level climate-change language a central public question. It does not establish the current workgroup’s motive. See the verified evidence note and project bibliography.

Register scope and method

2026-benchmark-register.csv contains all 1,512 unique leaf benchmark IDs in the three 2026 draft files: 279 K–5, 236 grades 6–8, and 997 grades 9–12. The corrected total includes 11 IDs whose PDF text contained internal spaces. The register candidate-screens all rows; current manual review classifies 1,468: 41 divergences, 3 feature-specific non-divergent comparisons, 355 high-confidence concept matches, and 1,069 medium-confidence concept matches. The remaining 44 are unresolved against the current research corpus. Concept matches do not make every dimension non-divergent; unresolved means the available sources do not support a reliable verdict, not that content is new or absent. See the full comparison’s coverage notes and graph source-coverage register for current counts and corpus limits.

The crosswalk below uses three statuses:

  • Non-divergent: the predecessor corpus clearly contains the same core instructional concept; no material difference in the feature described is established here.
  • Diverges: a grade placement, sequence, scope, or explicitness difference is visible in the cited benchmark text.
  • Unresolved vs research corpus: the available predecessor files or mapping are insufficient for a reliable conclusion.

These labels describe the specific comparison in each row, not the entire standard or its presumed drafting source. “Continuity” is not proof of textual borrowing.

Evolution and population change

Version Placement and sequence Normalized comparison Status
1996 Sunshine State Standards Grades 6–8: survival characteristics and fossil-record evidence of change (SC.F.2.3.3–.4); adaptations enhancing reproductive success (SC.G.1.3.2). Grades 9–12: mechanisms of change including mutation and natural selection, adaptation and diversity (SC.F.2.4.3); genetic variation and selection (SC.G.1.4.3). Fossil evidence and adaptation are in middle grades; mechanisms of evolutionary change are explicit in high school. Non-divergent with the broad two-stage progression in the 2026 draft.
1998/99 Grade Level Expectations Grade 6: adaptations aid survival. Grade 8: adaptations to environment and fossil-record change (SC.F.2.3.3–.4, GLE printed p.25). The 6th/7th GLE entries for fossil evidence explicitly defer content to grade 8. The GLE narrows placement inside the 1996 6–8 band. It does not specify a middle-school natural-selection mechanism in the reviewed 6–8 text. Diverges from 2008’s grade 7 natural-selection emphasis; aligns more closely with 2026’s grade 8 location for the explicit mechanism.
2008 NGSSS Grade 7: fossil evidence consistent with evolution (SC.7.L.15.1), genetic variation/environmental factors and natural selection (.15.2), and failure to adapt/extinction (.15.3), printed pp.48–49. High school: Big Idea 15 SC.912.L.15.1–.15, including evidence, speciation, Hardy–Weinberg, natural-selection conditions (.15.13) and other mechanisms (.15.14). Evolution mechanisms are named in grade 7 and revisited in a detailed high-school sequence. Diverges from 2026 by one grade for the explicit middle-school mechanism.
2026 review draft Grade 8: SC.8.SL.2.1–.2.4 covers selection conditions, inherited variation and survival/reproduction, population change over time, and natural vs artificial selection (draft pp.136–140); SC.8.SL.3.1–.3.2 addresses evidence and fossil-record change (pp.141–142). High school: SC.912.SL.6.1–.6.6 distributes variation, survival, reproductive success, population response, and explanations across a sequence (draft pp.199–203). Core selection concepts persist, with a shift from grade 7 in 2008 to grade 8 in 2026. The current draft gives grade 8 an explicit mechanism sequence and retains related population-change work in high school. “Natural selection” is explicit in the grade-8 strand; in the located high-school sequence it appears in a historical-development clarification (SC.912.SL.6.5 clarification 7), rather than as the named umbrella benchmark used in 2008. Diverges in grade placement and explicit explanatory scope; retained selection and population concepts do not make the full sequence equivalent.

Normalized takeaway: Compared with 2008, Florida moves the named middle-school natural-selection sequence from grade 7 to grade 8, while retaining fossil evidence and substantial high-school population-change content. Those retained elements do not resolve the specific omissions. No numbered 2026 benchmark uses evolution biologically; the only parent-level occurrence is the geological phrase “landscape evolution” in SC.912.EE.4; the located draft does not explicitly ask students to synthesize evidence and mechanisms through common descent, and it omits the 2008 high-school expectations about hominid ancestry and what hominid fossils show about human evolution (SC.912.L.15.10–.11). The 2008 text did not use the phrase “common descent” either, but it called evolution the organizing principle of life science and connected it to multiple lines of evidence. The 2026 benchmarks also do not locate several 2008 tasks on molecular-clock dating, reproductive isolation/speciation, Hardy–Weinberg calculations, genetic drift, and gene flow. The National Academies’ framework treats evolution as the unity and diversity of life, supported by shared ancestry and multiple lines of evidence. Without explicit synthesis, students may be taught selection, fossils, and relatedness as disconnected facts, and the work of supplying a coherent account—including humans’ place in evolutionary history—falls to local curriculum and teachers. That is a risk to accurate, consistent instruction, not proof that every classroom will omit these connections. The absence of a common-descent account is also sectarianly consequential; it does not establish the drafters’ intent. See the National Academies Framework and the sectarian-context analysis.

Climate and climate-system coverage

Version Placement and sequence Normalized comparison Status
1996 Sunshine State Standards Grades 9–12: changes in Earth’s climate, geology, and life forms can be traced and compared; Earth systems and organisms result from continuous change (SC.D.1.4.3–.4, printed p.2). Fossil-fuel combustion releases stored energy as heat and carbon dioxide (SC.G.2.4.1, printed p.3). Climate is one of several long-term Earth changes; CO₂ release is stated, but these cited benchmarks do not explicitly identify fossil-fuel CO₂ as a cause of contemporary global climate change. Non-divergent for climate-system change and carbon-emission concepts; attribution wording is not equivalent.
1998/99 GLEs Reviewed K and 6–8 GLE texts include climate as a habitat/geographic condition but no located “climate change,” “global warming,” or “greenhouse” benchmark language. Climate-change absence is limited to these surviving GLE files. These files do not document a grade-by-grade climate-change sequence. Unresolved for a complete 1998/99 climate crosswalk; the GLE corpus is partial and is not a replacement standards text.
2008 NGSSS Grade 6: water cycle/weather/climate, jet stream/ocean-current effects, and weather–climate distinction (SC.6.E.7.2–.6, printed p.43). High school: conditions shaping regional climate (SC.912.E.7.4), ocean influence on climate change through heat/carbon/water storage and movement (.7.6), and internal Earth-system plus external astronomical conditions contributing to global climate change (.7.7, printed pp.59–60). The 2008 text makes “climate change” and global-climate-change contributing conditions explicit in high school. Diverges from the 2026 climate strand’s primary framing as climate regulation and system interaction; note overlapping ocean/energy content.
2026 review draft Grade 6: solar energy/greenhouse role (SC.6.PE.1.2) and natural vs anthropogenic Earth-system changes (.1.3; draft p.73). Grade 8: weather/climate, climate-pattern factors and Earth-system interactions over time (SC.8.PE.1.1–.1.5, .2.1; pp.86–91); clarification explicitly includes natural processes and anthropogenic influences. High school: SC.912.PE.13.1–.13.6 covers radiation balance, greenhouse processes, atmospheric/ocean circulation, feedbacks, and integrated climate regulation (pp.143–146); SC.912.PE.16 addresses anthropogenic Earth-system effects (from p.155). Draft expands the mechanistic account of how climate is regulated and introduces natural/anthropogenic system distinctions by middle school. The high-school climate-regulation cluster says greenhouse-concentration changes affect climate but does not explicitly name human-caused global climate change in that cluster. Climate-related hazards and impacts are distributed elsewhere. Diverges in named scope and required task; substantial climate content remains, but the draft does not explicitly require several attribution and forecasting tasks.

Normalized takeaway: The draft retains substantial climate instruction: climate regulation, greenhouse processes, circulation, feedbacks, climate-pattern change, and human interactions with Earth systems. Grade 9–12 SC.912.PE.16.2 asks students to evaluate evidence about anthropogenic interactions with atmospheric and energy-related processes. But the draft does not repeat the 2008 tasks explicitly connecting ocean heat/carbon/water and astronomical conditions to global climate change (SC.912.E.7.6–.7), nor does it clearly require the NGSS task of evaluating evidence about causes of climate change over the past century (MS-ESS3-5) or using climate-model outputs to forecast rates and impacts (HS-ESS3-5). “Climate change” appears once in a benchmark clarification, not in a coded standard description or numbered benchmark; “global warming” does not appear in the grade-band documents. Those terminology changes reinforce the need to examine scope, but do not show that the subject is absent. Students could learn mechanisms and human-impact examples without being required to connect long-term observations, natural variability, human attribution, and projections in one evidence-based explanation. If aligned curriculum and assessment do not supply that synthesis, students must bridge the gaps themselves and classroom access may depend on local supplementation. That is a risk to coherent and accurate climate teaching, not a claim that climate science is absent or denied. The 2024 U.S. Climate Literacy Guide treats observation, modeling, natural variability, and human-caused recent change as connected elements of climate literacy. Corpus limits and source anchors

  • 1996: four surviving Florida standards PDFs cover PreK–2, 3–5, 6–8, and 9–12. The files are preserved scans/exports, not the complete original curriculum-framework publication. The 6–8 item is independently matched to an Internet Archive capture. Repository landing page: USF/FCIT Sunshine State Standards; archived 6–8 source: Internet Archive, science6.pdf.
  • 1998/99: retained files are Grade Level Expectations, not evidence of a separately adopted 1998 replacement standards set. The 6–8 GLE is cited by its printed page and benchmark ID; the unavailable high-school GLE prevents a complete vertical comparison. The contemporaneous 1998 review says the added grade expectations retained the 1996 benchmark statements; see ERIC ED439133.
  • 2008: local PDF is a mirror; FDOE confirms the adopted standards history at the official Florida science standards page. The benchmark citations above use the PDF’s printed page numbers, which differ by one from pdftotext page indexing in some locations.
  • 2026: draft is from FDOE’s standards review page. Page numbers above are PDF page indices matching the text extracts. The PDFs are drafts under review, not adopted standards.
  • There is no recovered full 2026 source DOCX/version history here. This appendix cannot establish who moved or revised the benchmarks, when those changes occurred, or which earlier text was used.

I human-checked the candidate rows below against source wording and current draft benchmark statements, related grade placements, and the register. The strongest specific 2008 carryover gaps concern astronomical forcing within global climate change; the integrated ocean–carbon–climate relationship; molecular-clock dating; reproductive isolation; Hardy–Weinberg calculations; hominid fossil evidence; genetic drift and gene flow; and selected genetics/biotechnology tasks such as DNA cloning, forensic identification, and genetic-code universality. Climate, evolution, population change, and genetics remain in the draft; these are candidate task or specificity gaps, not claims that the broad subjects were removed. Statuses are conservative:

  • Retained / relocated: the core concept has a plausible corresponding benchmark, possibly in a different grade or code.
  • Partial: a related concept remains, but the older/source benchmark’s specific framing, mechanism, evidence, or task is not explicit in the located current benchmark(s).
  • Possible omission: no corresponding benchmark was located for the specific concept/task after checking adjacent strands; absence remains provisional.
  • Unresolved: a reliable match or absence decision cannot be made from the available corpus.

Instructional-integrity implications of concentrated gaps

The gaps below are not all equally central, and a standards omission does not prove that a teacher will omit the concept. The concern is cumulative: when an official benchmark set names evidence, mechanisms, or system components without requiring students to connect them in an explanatory account, that synthesis becomes dependent on supplemental curriculum, assessment, and local teacher choices. Transferring that integration burden to students and teachers is not a sound design for equitable standards-based science learning, especially when it can make coherent instruction vary between classrooms. This is an interpretation of the benchmark set’s instructional signal, not a measured outcome or claim that the draft mandates inaccurate teaching. The National Academies’ K–12 framework calls for standards, curricula, instruction, and assessment to develop connected core ideas across grades.

  • Evolution: coded statements include natural selection, fossil evidence, and population change; supporting clarifications discuss phylogenetic relationships, but does not explicitly name common descent as the explanation that unifies evidence of life’s unity and diversity. It also omits the 2008 hominid-evolution expectations and does not locate several specific mechanisms or evidence methods, including reproductive isolation/speciation, genetic drift, gene flow, and molecular-clock dating. Collectively, these gaps can leave students with observations and mechanisms without an explicit benchmark-level synthesis explaining shared ancestry, human evolutionary history, and the different processes that shape populations. The National Academies’ framework identifies common ancestry and multiple converging lines of evidence within the core idea of biological evolution (Framework, Chapter 6); the external standard is a pedagogical comparator, not evidence of Florida access or authorship.
  • Climate: the Florida draft contains substantial climate mechanisms, system regulation, data work, and human Earth-system impacts. SC.912.PE.16.2 explicitly calls for evaluating evidence about anthropogenic interactions with atmospheric and energy-related processes. The gaps concern tasks that make a particular synthesis explicit: Florida 2008’s ocean–carbon–climate and astronomical-forcing expectations; the NGSS task asking students to evaluate evidence about the causes of climate change over the past century (MS-ESS3-5); and the NGSS task using climate-model outputs to forecast rates and impacts (HS-ESS3-5). The U.S. Global Change Research Program’s 2024 Climate Literacy Guide (accessed October 24, 2026) treats observations, modeling, natural variability, and human-caused recent change as connected parts of climate literacy. If standards and aligned assessments do not require students to integrate these pieces, students could learn accurate component facts without being asked to explain observed change or compare its drivers. That is a coherence and instructional-integrity risk, not evidence that climate science is absent or denied.

The standards packet alone cannot tell us whether adopted curricula, instructional materials, professional development, or assessments will supply these links. Reviewing those materials is therefore necessary before concluding that students actually receive fragmented or inaccurate instruction. The benchmark gaps nevertheless identify where the state should make the intended scientific synthesis explicit, rather than leaving each teacher to reconstruct it independently.

External-source lineage notes

  • California 2006 and Massachusetts 2006 are pre-NGSS state-framework comparators. Their shared reproductive-isolation/speciation concept also appears in the 2008 Florida standards, making the current-draft comparison useful for content tracking but not source attribution.
  • Massachusetts 2016 is an NGSS adaptation. Treat its shared wording/content as NGSS lineage unless a Massachusetts-specific difference is clearly at issue.
  • South Carolina 2014 is later than the 2007–08 Florida process. It is useful as a contemporary external comparator, not as evidence of access by the Florida workgroup.
  • Washington 2009 is a separate pre-NGSS state framework and a strong grade/topic/sequence comparator for Florida SC.6.EN.1.1–.3; the concepts are present in the draft, and the present evidence supports neither omission nor textual borrowing. The source review notes that Washington is not named in the preserved Florida 2007/2008 source lists.
  • South Carolina 2014 (H.B.4D, printed p.77 / PDF p.81) contains a content parallel to 2026 SC.912.CB.6.3 on regulated, accurate DNA replication. This is a candidate source to retain in future provenance work, but the short scientific proposition is not sufficiently distinctive to call a text lift; the 2014 date also rules it out as a source for the 2007–08 Florida standards.

The corpus records it as a plausible proposal available in the same general period, not proof it entered FDOE drafting.

Findings and confidence

  1. The strongest 2008 gap candidates remain specificity gaps, not wholesale subject omissions: astronomical forcing within global climate change (SC.912.E.7.7), Hardy–Weinberg quantitative population genetics (SC.912.L.15.12), named evolutionary mechanisms such as drift/gene flow (.15.14), DNA-cloning methods (.16.12), the near-universality of the genetic code (.16.9), and forensic DNA-identification methods (.16.11). The four-force comparison is another candidate scope reduction. Confidence is moderate that these exact tasks are not stated in reviewed draft benchmarks; lower that underlying ideas are absent from all instruction or supporting material.
  2. NGSS candidate differences include the explicit century-scale attribution and global-climate-model forecasting tasks (MS-ESS3-5, HS-ESS3-5), a kindergarten design/build challenge to reduce solar heating (K-PS3-2), the grade-3 design-merit operation for weather hazards (3-ESS3-1), the NGSS distinction between hazards that can be forecast and those that cannot (MS-ESS3-2), probabilistic weather forecasting (MS-ESS2-5), vibration as the cause of sound (1-PS4-1), a light/sound communication-device design challenge (1-PS4-4), and wave/particle model evaluation (HS-PS4-3). The 2026 kindergarten clarifications do cover illumination and material effects on light, so 1-PS4-2 and 1-PS4-3 are retained or shifted, not omissions. These are task-level differences: grade-8 Florida SC.8.PE.4.1 retains hazard data, risk assessment, and mitigation recommendations. Climate comparison across regions (3-ESS2-2) is retained in grades 4–5, and large-scale circulation in MS-ESS2-6 is present but moved to high school. The full item-by-item notes retain these distinctions and cross-reference sound’s causal-mechanism gap to earlier Florida GLEs.
  3. Evolution is retained, with explicit middle-school selection moving from grade 7 in 2008 to grade 8 in 2026. Several specialized high-school mechanisms or cases are candidates for omission, but no claim is made that “evolution” is absent.
  4. The 2008 task-equivalence review covers all 498 unique source rows: 234 retained/relocated, 227 partial, 35 possible omissions, and 2 unresolved. This combines the 171 IDs initially lacking direct anchors (46 retained/relocated, 94 partial, 29 possible omissions, 2 unresolved) and the 328 directly anchored IDs (188 retained/relocated, 134 partial, 6 possible omissions; the source-first slices overlap by one source ID, counted once in the combined total). Candidate clusters include nature-of-science outcomes (scientific vs. nonscientific questions, pseudoscience, theory/law distinctions, observation vs. inference, and model limits), plus specific methods or mechanisms in optics, physics, anatomy, evolution, and genetics. The six grade-band appendices preserve row-level rationale and locators.
  5. The NGSS inventory covers all 208 Performance Expectations in the preserved AllTopic document. The human-reviewed comparison table covers 12 representative PEs, including climate attribution/model forecasting, science/engineering design tasks, natural-hazard forecasting, circulation, evolution, quantum-model comparison, and elementary light/sound tasks. It is a selective audit, not a verdict on every PE or the detailed NGSS DCI, clarification, assessment-boundary, appendix, and evidence-statement material. The nearest-neighbor CSV is a discovery aid, not a complete or verified crosswalk.
  6. The further-external-source section is bounded to files already present in the corpus. Later or NGSS-adapted frameworks are comparators, not evidence of access during Florida’s 2007–08 process. Repeated concepts across related sources are not counted as independent pathways.
  7. The complete 196-row 1996/GLE review classified 114 as retained/relocated, 75 as partial, 7 as possible omissions, and none unresolved. The seven possible-omission source rows resolve to six concept clusters because 1996 SC.E.2.3 and GLE SC.E.2.3.1 repeat the same cross-galaxy commonality claim.

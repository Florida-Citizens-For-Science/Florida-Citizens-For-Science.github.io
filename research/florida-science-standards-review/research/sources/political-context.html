These omissions have a history

Florida has repeatedly fought over how public documents name evolution and climate change. That history makes the 2026 draft’s changes politically consequential. It does not, on its own, prove why the current writers made them.

2008: evolution wording was contested at adoption

Before the February 19, 2008 State Board vote, Florida’s science-writing group reviewed expert, public-meeting, and online input. Contemporary reporting and the National Center for Science Education describe a 4–3 adoption after a last-minute edit inserted “scientific theory of” before evolution and several other scientific theories. The qualifier was contested; the Board did not remove evolution. The adopted standard called evolution the fundamental concept underlying biology and cited multiple forms of evidence. The NCSE account, PBS NewsHour report, and FDOE adoption newsletter document that sequence.

The 2008 standards also explicitly taught climate change. High-school benchmark SC.912.E.7.7 asked students to identify and relate Earth-system and astronomical conditions contributing to global climate change. The adoption coverage reviewed here centers on evolution wording; it does not establish that climate-change language was a comparable point of dispute in the 2008 standards vote.

2011–2017: reports of climate-language restrictions in state agencies

Former Florida Department of Environmental Protection employees told the Florida Center for Investigative Reporting and WLRN that supervisors warned them not to use terms including “climate change,” “global warming,” and “sea-level rise” in official work. The 2015 report traced one warning to a 2011 staff meeting after Rick Scott took office. DEP denied that it had a policy. A 2017 Sun Sentinel investigation, summarized by Columbia Law School’s Sabin Center, reported that the restrictions reached other agencies. These are documented news reports of employee accounts; the review has not located a written statewide order.

2020–2024: health messaging and climate education under DeSantis

A Sun Sentinel investigation reported that Florida health officials were told not to discuss COVID-19 publicly before the November 2020 election. CBS Miami’s interview with reporter Mario Ariza describes the reporting. This concerned public-health communications; it was not a climate-language directive.

In 2024, the Orlando Sentinel and later WUSF/The Hechinger Report documented state requests to revise climate material in science textbooks, including reducing coverage and demanding citations for statements about human-caused climate change. Separately, DeSantis signed Chapter 2024-186, which revised energy statutes and removed climate-change findings from state law. That law changed energy policy; it did not amend the K–12 science standards.

Why this matters for the 2026 draft

The 2026 draft does not use “evolution” in any numbered benchmark and no longer states it as biology’s organizing idea; related material on natural selection, fossils, and evolutionary relationships remains. “Climate change” appears once in a supporting clarification, not in a numbered benchmark; extensive climate mechanisms and human Earth-system interactions remain, while some explicit attribution and synthesis tasks in earlier standards are not repeated.

Given the history of political disputes over these exact terms and topics, describing the changes as neutral or self-explanatory would ignore relevant evidence. The record supports treating them as headline findings and asking the state for its rationale. It does not establish that the 2026 workgroup acted to suppress either subject. Dated drafts, meeting records, source notes, and comment responses are needed to test that explanation.
